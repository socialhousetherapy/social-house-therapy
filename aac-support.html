AAC support

AAC: Giving Every Child a Way to Be Heard

Augmentative and alternative communication is any method of communicating that is not speech: gestures, signs, pictures, boards, books, and speech-generating devices. It is not a last resort, it is not an admission that speech has failed, and it does not have to be earned. It is a way for a person to say what they think, starting now.

This guide covers what AAC actually includes, the myths that keep children waiting for years (the research here is unusually unanimous), who benefits, how a system gets chosen and by whom (you, with our help, and never a sales rep, because we do not sell devices), what vocabulary matters, and the single most important thing families can do: model on it constantly, without asking anything in return.

01What AAC is

Everything that carries a message when speech cannot

AAC is usually divided into unaided methods, which use only the body, and aided methods, which use something external. Almost every AAC user relies on a combination, and that is the goal rather than a compromise. Most people already use AAC daily without calling it that: pointing at a menu item in a loud restaurant, texting instead of calling, waving from across a street.

01

Unaided: gestures, signs, and the body

Pointing, reaching, facial expression, body language, and manual signs. Always available, never needs charging, and works in the bath and the swimming pool. The limitation is that only people who know the signs can understand them, which restricts who a child can talk to.

02

Low tech: boards, books, and cards

Printed communication boards, symbol books, choice cards, alphabet boards, and partner-assisted scanning for children who cannot point directly. Cheap, robust, and immediate. Every child who uses a device should also have a low-tech backup, because devices break, run out of battery, and get left at school.

03

High tech: speech-generating devices

Dedicated devices or apps running on a tablet, which speak aloud when the user selects words. The spoken output matters, because it means the child can be understood by anyone rather than only by people who have learned their system, and it lets them call out across a room.

  • Touch access, directly on screen
  • Keyguards for more accurate selection
  • Switch access with scanning
  • Eye gaze access
  • Head pointing
  • Adjustable grid sizes and symbol sets
04

Text-based systems

For literate users, typing with word prediction is fast and unlimited. Literacy instruction is therefore part of AAC work, not separate from it: a child who can spell has access to every word in the language rather than only the ones someone thought to program.

“Multimodal is the goal. A child should be able to sign, point, speak, and use a device, and choose whichever one fits the moment.”

02Myths that cost children years

The beliefs that delay AAC, and what the evidence says

This section matters more than any other on the page. Every one of these ideas is still repeated by well-meaning professionals, and each one costs children months or years of not being able to say what they think.

01

“It will stop them from talking.”

This is the most common fear, and it is also the best-answered question in our field. The landmark review tracked speech before, during, and after AAC across 27 rigorously studied cases: 89% talked more after AAC arrived, 11% stayed the same, and exactly zero talked less. A second review, in autism specifically, found the same one-way pattern. The gains were typically modest (AAC is a voice, not a speech cure), but the direction never reversed. The mechanism is not mysterious: AAC lowers frustration, multiplies successful conversations, and pairs the spoken word with a symbol every single time.

02

“They need to prove they are ready.”

There are no prerequisites for AAC. Not cause and effect, not matching pictures, not a certain cognitive level, not the ability to point accurately. Those things develop through using a communication system, not before it. Requiring a child to demonstrate skills in order to earn a voice has it exactly backwards.

03

“They're too young.”

AAC can start in the toddler years, and there is no minimum age. In a randomized trial, toddlers with fewer than ten spoken words who got a speech-generating device ended up with more spoken words than the speech-only group, not fewer. Typically developing children marinate in language for about eighteen months before saying much of anything; an AAC learner deserves the same head start, not a waiting list.

04

“It's too late for them.”

It is not. People of every age learn to use AAC, and there is no age at which communication stops being worth building. Late is worse than early. Late is much better than never.

05

“It's a last resort, after speech therapy fails.”

AAC is not the opposite of speech therapy, it is part of it. Working on speech and providing AAC happen at the same time, for the same reason: the child needs to be able to communicate today while speech continues to develop.

06

“They talk, so they don't need it.”

Speaking children sometimes need AAC too. Speech may be unreliable when a child is overwhelmed, shut down, unwell, or in an unfamiliar place. Others speak clearly at home and not at all at school. Having a backup for the moments when words are not available is not a downgrade.

07

“They just need pictures of things they want.”

A system of nouns and snack pictures is a vending machine, not a voice. A child needs to be able to comment, refuse, joke, complain, ask questions, and say something nobody expected. That requires core vocabulary and a system built for generating new sentences.

08

“They aren't using it, so it isn't working.”

When a system is not being used, the usual cause is not the child. It is insufficient modeling by the people around them, a system that cannot say enough, limited access, or a lack of real reasons to communicate. Check those four things before concluding the child is not an AAC user.

03Who benefits

AAC is for anyone whose speech does not meet their needs

That is the whole criterion. It does not depend on diagnosis, and two children with the same diagnosis may need very different systems or none at all.

Childhood apraxia of speech Autism Cerebral palsy Down syndrome Genetic conditions Developmental language disorder Hearing loss Brain injury Progressive conditions Temporary loss of speech Situational mutism Severe speech sound disorders

A useful way to decideAsk whether your child can currently say everything they might want to say, to anyone they might want to say it to, in every place they go. If the answer is no, AAC is worth assessing.

The Communication Bill of Rights. Widely adopted in our field, it states that every person has the right to make requests and refuse things, to express preferences and feelings, to be spoken to with respect, to be addressed directly rather than talked about, and to have access to functional communication at all times. Those rights do not depend on a child demonstrating anything first.

04Choosing a system

How the right system gets identified

There is no Best Device, whatever a Facebook group may have told you. There is only the best match between a specific person, their environments, the things they need to say, and the features available. Finding that match is what an AAC evaluation is for. Ours follows the feature-matching approach in ASHA's guidance, and it involves trialing real systems rather than choosing from a catalogue.

1Understand the personCurrent communication, language, vision, hearing, motor skills, positioning, literacy, preferences, and what they want to be able to say.
2Understand the environmentsHome, school, car, playground, pool, grandparents. Who the communication partners are and what they can support.
3Define the tasksWhat does this person actually need to do? Order food, argue with a sibling, answer in class, tell a joke, say they are in pain.
4Match the featuresAccess method, grid size, symbol set, vocabulary organization, voice, durability, mounting, and how it grows over time.
5Trial before decidingReal use, in real places, with the people who will support it. Trials frequently change the answer.

Things that get weighed

  • How the child will select: touch, switch, eye gaze, or partner-assisted
  • Symbol size and how many appear on a page
  • Whether the vocabulary layout stays consistent as it grows
  • Motor planning: does a word stay in the same place forever
  • How much can be said, not just how easy it is to start
  • Durability, waterproofing, and mounting
  • Battery life and charging routines
  • Voice options the child actually likes
  • What the school can support
  • Funding and documentation requirements

On consistencyWhere a word lives on the screen matters enormously. Systems that keep words in the same place as vocabulary expands let a child build motor memory, the same way a touch typist stops thinking about the letters. Rearranging a layout is not a small change.

Who picks the device? You do.

Our job is the clinical part: working out, through assessment and trials, which features your child needs, and putting it in writing. The decision belongs to your family. We do not sell devices, we take no commissions, and no manufacturer buys our lunch, so if we get enthusiastic about a system, that is a clinical opinion, not a pitch. What we bring instead:

  • A written feature recommendation: what to look for, in plain English and in the language funding reviewers expect
  • Introductions to the right people: the local reps for the major device companies, who run demos and handle funding paperwork, and AzTAP, Arizona's assistive technology program, which loans devices for short home trials at no cost and even covers the shipping
  • A sounding board during trials: the first pick is not always the keeper, and changing your mind is the whole point of trialing
  • Everything after the box arrives: therapy on the system, family coaching, and school coordination, which is where a device becomes a voice

The short versionWe recommend, you decide, and nobody here makes a dime either way. Our only stake is that your child ends up with a voice they actually use.

05Words that matter

Core words are the ones that do the work

A tiny set of words does almost all the talking. In the study our field quotes constantly, researchers recorded 50 toddlers at play and at snack time and found that just 23 words made up 96% of everything said. Not dinosaur. Not trampoline. Words like go, stop, more, want, that, no, help, my, mine, in. Core words are abstract, they work in every situation, and they combine to say almost anything.

Core wordsvsFringe words
go, more, stop, that, helpvsdinosaur, trampoline, grandma

A few hundred wordsvsThousands, added as needed

Both are needed. A system heavy on fringe words and light on core produces a child who can request a snack and cannot tell you their stomach hurts, that they want a different one, or that you got it wrong.

Communication is not just requesting

Requesting is where most AAC programs start and it is not where they should stop. A full communication system needs to support all the reasons people talk.

Requesting things and actions Refusing and protesting Commenting on the world Asking questions Greeting and small talk Making jokes Complaining Expressing feelings Telling someone what happened Arguing and disagreeing Asking for help Saying they are hurt or unwell Choosing and changing their mind Telling someone to stop

Program the difficult words. A system should include no, stop, I don't like it, go away, and that hurts. Communication that only permits agreement is not communication, and the ability to refuse is a safeguarding matter as much as a language one.

06Making it work at home

What families actually do, day to day

01

Model on the system, constantly

This is the whole ballgame. Point to words on your child's system while you talk, all day, for your own messages, without asking them to do anything. You do not need to hit every word in your sentence. One or two key words is plenty.

  • Say “let's go” and touch go
  • Say “I want more coffee” and touch more
  • Say “that's different” and touch different
  • Say “I don't like that” and touch like and not

The math on modelingBy 18 months, a typical toddler has soaked up roughly 4,380 waking hours of spoken language. An AAC learner whose only models come from two 30-minute therapy sessions a week would need 84 years to match that. Nobody is asking you for 4,380 hours: just narrate life on the device while you live it.

02

Do not quiz, and do not require

“Show me ball” and “tell me what you want first” turn the device into a test. Model without expectation. If your child is not responding, model more rather than demanding more.

03

Keep the system available every waking hour

Not just at the table or in therapy. In the car, at the park, in the bath with a laminated board, at grandma's house, at the doctor. Communication needs are not scheduled, and a system that is only sometimes there teaches a child not to rely on it.

04

Never remove it as a consequence

A device is a voice. Removing it as punishment, or because a child is using it repetitively, is not a behavior strategy. If a child is using the system in a way that concerns you, the useful question is what they are trying to do and what they need instead.

05

Wait longer than feels comfortable

Composing a message on a device takes far longer than speaking one, particularly with switch or eye gaze access. Adults fill the silence, guess the message, or move on. Count to at least ten, keep your face interested, and let the person finish.

06

Respond to what was said, not how

If your child hits go outside, go outside if you possibly can. Honoring messages is what teaches a child that the system works. Correcting grammar, asking for a full sentence, or praising the pressing instead of answering the message all undermine that lesson.

07

Avoid hand-over-hand

Physically moving a child's hand to a symbol produces a message the child did not choose and teaches them to wait for someone to move them. Model on the system yourself instead, or offer hand-under-hand support, where the child rests their hand on yours and controls the movement.

08

Build in real reasons to communicate

Offer choices, pause a favorite activity, do something silly and wrong on purpose, leave a step out of a routine, and give small portions. Then wait, model an option, and wait again. A child who has no reason to communicate will not, whatever system they have.

09

Teach everyone in the child's life

Siblings, grandparents, teachers, aides, babysitters, coaches. Communication partner training is one of the strongest predictors of whether AAC succeeds, and it is where most implementations quietly fail. Siblings are often the fastest learners and the most motivating partners.

10

Have a routine for charging, carrying, and backup

The dull logistics decide whether AAC survives contact with real life. Charge it in the same place every night, agree who carries it between home and school, keep a printed board in the car and in the bag, and know who to call when it breaks.

Have a device that is not being used?

This is extremely common and it is almost never the child's fault. We can look at the system, the vocabulary, the access, and the modeling, and work out what needs to change.

07What therapy looks like

AAC therapy is language therapy that happens on a system

Getting a device is the beginning, not the outcome. The work is teaching language, teaching the people around the child, and building the communication into ordinary life.

01

Assessment and trials

Finding the match, not picking a product.

An AAC evaluation looks at current communication, language, symbol understanding, vision and motor access, positioning, communication partners, environments, and vocabulary needs, and then trials systems. It often continues across more than one appointment, and the report includes the documentation funding sources typically ask for.

02

Teaching language

Same language goals, different channel.

  • Building core vocabulary use
  • Combining words into new messages
  • Expanding beyond requesting
  • Navigating between pages efficiently
  • Repairing a misunderstanding
  • Growing grammar on the system
  • Literacy and spelling as a route to any word
  • Self-advocacy and explaining the system to others
03

Partner training

Coaching the people who are there all week.

We teach caregivers, siblings, teachers, and aides how to model, how long to wait, what not to do, and how to keep the system available. With your written permission we coordinate directly with schools and other providers so that one approach follows the child across settings.

04

Keeping it working

Vocabulary, settings, and access change as the child does.

Systems need maintenance: new vocabulary for new interests, adjusted grid sizes, changed access as motor skills develop, and updated pages for new environments. We also revisit whether the system is still the right one, because children outgrow systems the way they outgrow shoes.

A note from Madison

The moment that never gets old is when a child says something nobody was expecting. Not a request, not a label, but an opinion, a joke, or a complaint about something that happened three days ago. That is when families realize how much was in there the whole time, waiting for a way out. It is the reason I do not wait to introduce AAC, and the reason I get impatient with anyone who wants a child to prove they are ready first.
