Early communication and late talkers

Late Talkers and Early Communication: What to Watch For and What Actually Helps

A child who is not talking yet is still communicating. Before first words arrive, children build an entire foundation underneath them: understanding, attention, imitation, play, gestures, and the discovery that communication does something. When words are slow to come, the useful question is not only how many words, but which parts of that foundation are in place.

This guide covers what early communication actually includes, what is typical at each age, which signs deserve a closer look, why some children talk later than their peers, and the specific things families can start doing at home today. It ends with what speech therapy looks like for this age group and how our practice approaches it.

01More than words

Talking is the last floor of a building that has been going up for a year.

Spoken words are the most visible part of communication, which is why they are what families count. But words sit on top of a set of earlier skills, and a child who is not yet talking may be strong in some of those areas and thin in others. That difference matters more than the word count, because it changes what kind of help is useful.

The four things we are really watching

01

How much does the child understand of what is said to them?

02

How often do they try to communicate, and for how many different reasons?

03

What tools are they using: sounds, gestures, signs, words, or a device?

04

Are they learning new communication over time, or has progress stalled?

Communication has reasons, and the range of reasons matters

A child who only communicates to request things has a narrower system than a child who also protests, greets, comments, shows off, asks, and jokes. Two children can have the same twenty words and be in very different places. When we assess a young child, we look for the range of communicative functions, not just the vocabulary list.

  • Requesting an object or action
  • Refusing or protesting
  • Getting attention
  • Greeting and saying goodbye
  • Commenting on something interesting
  • Showing an object to share it
  • Asking for help
  • Asking a question
  • Answering
  • Labeling and naming
  • Requesting more or again
  • Being silly on purpose

Try this tonightFor one day, jot down every time your child communicates something and what it was for. Most families are surprised twice: first by how many attempts there are, and then by how many of them are requests.

02What is typical

Ages and stages, with the honest amount of variation

Milestones describe what most children do by a given age. They are useful as a screening tool and unhelpful as a scoreboard. Children arrive at these skills in slightly different orders and at slightly different speeds, and a child who is a few weeks behind on one item is in a completely different situation than a child who is missing a whole layer.

01

Birth to 6 months
  • Quiets or turns toward voices
  • Smiles socially
  • Makes cooing and vowel-like sounds
  • Laughs and squeals
  • Watches faces closely
  • Different cries for different needs
  • Starts to babble with early consonants
  • Notices toys that make sounds

The pointLong before words, babies are practicing turn-taking. You make a sound, they make a sound. That back-and-forth is the same structure conversation will use later.

02

6 to 12 months
  • Babbles strings of consonants: bababa, dadada
  • Responds to their own name
  • Understands “no” and a few familiar words
  • Looks where you point
  • Points, reaches, or shows to communicate
  • Waves, claps, or lifts arms to be picked up
  • Plays peekaboo and other social games
  • Uses one or two words around the first birthday

Watch for gesturesGestures are the strongest early predictor most families can actually observe. A child who points, shows, gives, and waves is telling you the communication system is switched on, even if words are not there yet.

03

12 to 18 months
  • About 50 words by 18 months is a common benchmark, and it sits below the median rather than at it
  • Follows simple one-step directions
  • Points to a few body parts or pictures
  • Brings objects to show you
  • Imitates sounds, words, and actions
  • Uses a word plus a gesture together
  • Understands many more words than they say
  • Enjoys simple pretend play like feeding a doll

The gap between understanding and speaking is normal at this age. Comprehension usually runs well ahead of production, and that is a good sign rather than a problem.

04

18 to 24 months
  • Vocabulary grows quickly, often in a burst
  • At least 50 words by 24 months
  • Begins two-word combinations: more juice, daddy go
  • Uses words for more than requesting
  • Follows directions without gestures
  • Points to pictures in a book when named
  • Familiar adults understand a good deal; strangers understand much less
  • Imitates new words readily

The 24-month lineFewer than 50 words, or no two-word combinations, at 24 months is the most widely used flag for a closer look. It is a cut-point, not an average, and it is set deliberately low. If you have been confused by the CDC checklist saying something different, or by the vocabulary numbers floating around online, the next section explains why they disagree.

05

2 to 3 years
  • Hundreds of words and growing weekly
  • Two- and three-word phrases become the norm
  • Asks simple questions
  • Follows two-step directions
  • Uses early grammar: plurals, -ing, simple pronouns
  • Names familiar objects and pictures
  • Roughly half of single words are clear to unfamiliar listeners by about 31 months
  • Play becomes more imaginative and sequenced

Three different kinds of number, and why they disagree

Almost every argument about toddler vocabulary is really two people quoting different statistics at each other. Fifty words, three hundred words, and the CDC checklist are all correct. They are answering different questions.

01
The median: the middle child

Half of children are above it and half below, by definition. Parent-report norms like the MacArthur-Bates CDI and the open Wordbank database report medians, and the middle 24-month-old produces somewhere around 300 words. A median is a description, not a target. Sitting under it is not a finding.

02
The CDC benchmark: what most children do

The CDC revised its milestone checklists in 2022 (Zubler et al., Pediatrics) to list what at least 75 percent of children can do by a given age, replacing the older 50th-percentile standard. That one change is why CDC lists 50 words at 30 months rather than 24. It also means a missed CDC item places a child in the bottom quarter, which is worth a conversation rather than a shrug.

03
The screening cut-point: the line that triggers a look

Fewer than 50 words, or no two-word combinations, at 24 months is the threshold used in late-talker research and in most clinics. It sits far below the median, roughly in the bottom 10 to 15 percent. It is engineered to catch children who need a second look, not to describe the average two-year-old.

04
The average: usually a red flag on the source

Means are rarely used for early vocabulary, because the distribution is lopsided. A handful of very high talkers pull the mean well above the median, so an average overstates what a typical child does. If you find an average vocabulary figure online with no source attached, it is usually a median wearing a disguise.

The practical versionCompare your child to the cut-point, not to the median. Half of typical two-year-olds sit below the median and always will. That is what a median means.

So what is a “late talker” exactly?

The term describes a specific pattern: a child roughly 18 to 30 months old whose spoken vocabulary is well below expectations for their age, while understanding, play, social interaction, motor skills, and thinking all appear to be developing typically. It is a description, not a diagnosis. It says where a child is right now, not what will happen next.

The distinctions in that list are exactly what a good evaluation sorts out, and they change the plan completely. A child who has plenty of language but struggles to physically produce words needs different help from a child who is still building the language itself.

03Signs to look at

When it is worth having someone look

Any one item below can occur in a child who is developing perfectly well. Patterns are what matter. If several of these describe your child, or if one of them is the kind of thing that keeps surfacing in your mind, that is enough reason to ask.

Reach out promptly if there is

  • No babbling with consonant sounds by around 10 months
  • No pointing, waving, showing, or other gestures by 12 months
  • No words by 16 months
  • Fewer than 50 words, or no two-word combinations, by 24 months
  • Any loss of words, gestures, or social skills the child previously had, at any age
  • Little or no response to their name by 12 months
  • Concern about hearing, or a history of frequent ear infections

Loss of skills is different. A child who had words or gestures and then stopped using them should be evaluated promptly rather than monitored. This is one of the few items on the page that is genuinely time-sensitive.

Worth mentioning at your next visit

  • Communicates mostly by leading you by the hand
  • Rarely imitates sounds, words, or actions
  • Uses very few different consonant sounds
  • Vocabulary has plateaued for several months
  • Communicates almost only to request things
  • Understands much less than same-age peers
  • Gets frustrated frequently when trying to communicate
  • Prefers to play alone with objects rather than with people
  • Struggles with pretend play
  • Has trouble with foods, textures, or chewing as well
  • Family history of speech, language, or reading difficulty
  • You keep being told to wait and it does not sit right

“Parents are usually right that something is different. Where they need help is knowing what it is and how much it matters.”

What we look at alongside talking

Speech does not develop in a vacuum. When a young child is slow to talk, several nearby areas are worth checking, because any of them can be part of the story.

01

Hearing

Even mild or fluctuating hearing loss, including the kind that comes with repeated ear infections and fluid, can blur the speech sounds a child hears during the exact window when they are learning them. A newborn hearing screen passed two years ago does not rule out hearing loss today. Current audiology testing is often the single most useful step for a late talker.

02

Understanding

Comprehension is easy to overestimate at home, because children are excellent at using routines, gestures, tone of voice, and context to work out what is expected. We test understanding in ways that remove those clues, because a child whose understanding is also delayed needs a different plan than a child who understands everything and cannot yet say it.

03

Social connection and play

Joint attention, imitation, turn-taking, and pretend play are the engine of early language learning. When those are thin, vocabulary teaching alone tends not to stick, and the plan needs to start further back. This is also where we consider whether a broader developmental evaluation would be useful.

04

Speech sounds and motor planning

Some children have plenty to say and real difficulty getting their mouth to produce it. Signs include a very small inventory of consonants, vowel distortions, words that come out differently every attempt, and visible effort or groping. When this pattern appears, motor speech assessment is added rather than assuming a language delay.

05

Feeding and oral skills

Feeding and speech use overlapping structures, and difficulty with textures, chewing, or drinking sometimes shows up alongside slow speech development. It does not cause the delay, but it is worth noting, and it may point toward areas that deserve their own look.

04Why it happens

Why some children talk later

For most late talkers, there is no single identifiable cause, and that is a genuine finding rather than a failure to look hard enough. Language development is influenced by genetics, hearing, neurological development, temperament, medical history, and experience, and those threads are usually tangled together.

  • Family history of late talking or language difficulty
  • Differences in how the brain builds language
  • Hearing loss, including temporary or fluctuating loss
  • Frequent ear infections during the first two years
  • Prematurity or early medical complications
  • Motor planning difficulty affecting speech production
  • Autism or another developmental difference
  • Genetic conditions affecting development
  • Reduced opportunity for responsive back-and-forth interaction
  • Very high screen exposure displacing interaction
  • Temperament, including a cautious approach to new skills
  • Often, no identifiable cause at all

“A cause is nice to have. A plan is what changes the next six months.”

We look carefully for anything treatable or important to identify. When nothing turns up, the intervention is the same: build the skills the child needs next, and give the family the tools to keep building between sessions.

One thing worth naming. Nothing you did caused this. Late talking is not the result of one caregiver's speaking style, working outside the home, a second child arriving, or any of the other things parents quietly blame themselves for. What you do next matters. What you did before almost certainly did not cause it.

05What helps at home

Twelve strategies that actually move the needle

These are the same techniques we teach parents in therapy, and they are effective precisely because they happen hundreds of times a week rather than once. None of them require materials, a program, or a set-aside practice time. They work by changing the shape of interactions you are already having.

01

Get face to face, and get low

Sit on the floor, lie down, or pull your chair around so your face is at your child's eye level. Children learn words partly by watching mouths, and being face to face makes you far easier to notice and imitate. It also makes their communication attempts far easier for you to catch.

What it looks like

  • Play on the floor rather than supervising from the couch
  • Sit across from the highchair rather than beside it
  • Hold a toy near your face when you name it
02

Follow their lead instead of directing

Children learn words fastest for things they are already interested in at that exact moment. If your child is lining up cars, talk about cars. If they are dumping a bin, talk about dumping. Redirecting a toddler to your chosen teaching activity usually costs you the attention you needed.

In practiceJoin the activity they have chosen and add language to it. You are not abandoning the plan. Their interest is the plan.

03

Wait longer than feels natural

This is the strategy families underestimate most. After you say something, or after your child looks at something they want, pause. Lean in, look expectant, and silently count to ten. Adults usually leave about a second before filling the silence themselves, which is nowhere near enough for a child who is still assembling a response.

Where to use the pause

  • After asking anything
  • Before handing over a requested item
  • In the middle of a familiar song
  • Between steps of a routine
  • After your child makes a sound
  • Any time you want a turn from them
04

Narrate what is happening

Talk about what your child is doing as they do it, and what you are doing as you do it. This puts words directly onto the experience the child is having, which is how vocabulary attaches. Keep sentences short and concrete: washing hands. Water on. Soap. Rub rub rub. All done.

NoteThis is not the same as talking constantly. A steady stream of adult speech is background noise. Short phrases, matched to the moment, with pauses in between, is what children can actually use.

05

Comment far more than you question

Questions like what's this? and can you say ball? put a child on the spot and mostly test what they already know. Comments give them new language for free. Aim for roughly four comments per question. That ratio is a clinical rule of thumb rather than a research finding, but the principle under it is well supported: children learn more from language offered than from language demanded.

What's that?tryA big red truck!
Say “juice”tryJuice. You want juice.
What color is it?tryThat one is blue.
06

Expand what they give you

Take whatever your child communicates and give it back one small step longer. If they say car, you say big car or car go. If they point at the door, you say go outside. One step, not five. The goal is a model just above where they are, not a sentence they cannot use.

Why it worksExpansions show the child exactly what their own idea sounds like with a little more language attached, at the moment they are most invested in that idea.

07

Say the same words many, many times

Children usually need to hear a word many times, in many situations, before they use it. Pick five to ten useful words for the week and load the environment with them. Useful early words are ones that work everywhere: more, go, up, open, help, all done, mine, uh oh, again, no.

Good early word choices

  • Words that work across many activities
  • Words that get something to happen
  • Words the child already tries to say
  • Words with sounds the child already makes
  • Names of people they love
  • Words for favorite objects and foods
08

Build in reasons to communicate

If everything is handed over before it is requested, there is no reason to ask. Gently engineer small moments where communication is the obvious tool, then accept any form of communication as a valid answer.

  • Put a favorite item in view but out of reach
  • Use clear containers with tight lids
  • Give a small portion so more is needed
  • Offer two choices and hold both up
  • Pause a favorite song mid-line
  • “Forget” a step in a familiar routine
  • Hand over something silly on purpose
  • Start a game they love and then stop

Do not hold things hostage. Waiting expectantly for a few seconds is a strategy. Refusing to give a child something until they produce a word is not, and it usually produces tears rather than language.

09

Use routines and songs with predictable gaps

Repeated routines are language gold, because the child already knows what comes next and can devote all their attention to the words. Bath, meals, diaper changes, getting in the car, and bedtime are all scripts. So are songs and rhymes, and the pause before the predictable part is one of the strongest invitations to talk there is.

Ready, set… then wait. One, two… then wait. Twinkle twinkle little… then wait.

10

Honor every form of communication

Respond to gestures, sounds, signs, pictures, and device messages as real communication, then model the spoken word alongside them. Children who have a working way to communicate become more communicative overall, not less. Signs and other supports build the same language system speech runs on.

The evidenceSign and picture supports do not delay speech. Millar, Light and Schlosser's 2006 meta-analysis found that across the best-documented cases, 89 percent gained speech, 11 percent showed no change, and none declined. The gains were modest, so this is not a shortcut to talking. But the fear that runs the other way has no evidence behind it at all.

11

Read together, but do not just read the words

For a late talker, the printed text is the least useful part of a book. Label pictures, make sound effects, point, ask an occasional simple question, let your child turn pages and choose the same book for the fortieth time. Board books with clear single images beat long stories at this stage.

  • Talk about the pictures instead of reading the sentences
  • Let the child control the pace and the page
  • Repeat the same books often, on purpose
  • Make the animal sounds and the crashes
  • Connect the book to real life afterward
12

Protect interaction time from screens

Screens are not the villain in every story, and no family needs to be perfect about this. The mechanism is simple though: very young children learn language from responsive back-and-forth with people, and screen time mostly displaces that. Background television also reduces how much adults and children talk to each other, even when nobody is watching it.

  • Keep the television off when nobody is watching it
  • Video calls with familiar people are interactive and are fine
  • If a show is on, watch together and talk about it
  • Protect meals and the bedtime routine as screen-free interaction time

“Change the interaction, not the child. Every one of these strategies works by giving a child more chances to communicate and more useful models of how.”

06Myths worth retiring

Advice you will hear, and what the evidence says

01

“Boys talk later.”

There is something to this, and it is the opposite of reassuring. Zubrick's 2007 study of 1,766 two-year-olds found male sex to be a genuine predictor of late language emergence, so boys really are somewhat more likely to start late. But Fisher's 2017 meta-analysis found that sex did not predict which late talkers caught up. Being a boy raises the odds of arriving at this page and tells you nothing about how it ends. Used as a reason to wait, it means the group most likely to need help is the least likely to get it early.

02

“Einstein didn't talk until he was four.”

The story is almost certainly embellished, and one anecdote from a century ago is not a basis for a decision about your child. Some late talkers become highly verbal adults. That is a fact about the group, not a prediction about the individual sitting in front of you.

03

“They'll grow out of it. Just wait and see.”

Most late talkers do close the gap. Roughly a fifth to a quarter do not, and go on to language difficulties that affect literacy and school; Fisher's 2017 meta-analysis puts persistent difficulty at about 5 to 7 percent of the general population. There is a quieter finding alongside it: even children who move into the normal range tend, as a group, to score a little below peers on language measures for years afterward. Nobody can reliably tell in advance which child is which, which is the whole problem with the advice. Since early support is low-risk and builds skills either way, waiting buys very little and can cost a year.

04

“Sign language will stop them from talking.”

The opposite is closer to the truth. Communication builds communication. A child with a reliable way to be understood experiences fewer breakdowns, stays in more interactions, and gets more language practice, not less.

05

“They don't need to talk because you anticipate everything.”

There is a grain of truth here, and it gets weaponized against parents. Building in a few extra communication opportunities is genuinely useful. But responsive, attentive parenting does not cause language delay, and no child who is able to talk stays silent because life is too convenient.

06

“It's the tongue tie.”

Tongue tie can affect feeding in infancy and occasionally affects specific speech sounds. It very rarely explains why a toddler is not talking, since talking is a language skill before it is a tongue skill. Be cautious of any plan that leads with a procedure before an evaluation.

07

“Bilingual homes cause delays.”

They do not. Bilingual children hit early milestones on a comparable schedule when both languages are counted together, and mixing languages is a normal feature of bilingual development rather than confusion. Dropping a home language usually costs a family its closeness with grandparents and community without helping the child at all. Keep both.

07What therapy looks like

Therapy for a toddler looks like play, because that is what works

Early language therapy is not drills at a table. For this age group, the evidence points toward naturalistic, play-based intervention delivered in familiar routines, with a significant portion of the work aimed at the adults who are with the child the other one hundred and sixty-seven hours of the week.

01

Understanding first

We start by finding out what is actually holding words back.

An evaluation establishes whether understanding, attention, play, imitation, gestures, motor speech, or hearing is part of the picture. That determines where therapy starts, which is often earlier in the sequence than families expect and is why progress can be faster than it looks.

02

In session

Play with a purpose behind every choice.

Sessions look relaxed and are tightly planned. Toys are chosen because they create repeated opportunities for target words. Activities are arranged so the child has reasons to communicate. The clinician models constantly, waits deliberately, and tracks how many opportunities and responses actually occurred.

  • Building joint attention and shared enjoyment
  • Increasing the number of communication attempts
  • Widening the reasons a child communicates
  • Growing the sound inventory
  • Teaching a core set of powerful early words
  • Moving from single words toward combinations
  • Strengthening imitation and play skills
  • Adding signs, pictures, or a device where useful
03

Caregiver coaching

The strategies have to leave the session with you.

We teach you the techniques directly, practice them with you during your own routines, and troubleshoot what did not work last week. This is not homework in the school sense. It is the mechanism by which an hour a week turns into daily practice. Roberts and Kaiser's 2011 meta-analysis found that parent-implemented language intervention produced significant gains in children's receptive and expressive language, which is why we structure sessions this way rather than working with your child in a separate room.

Watch it modeledThe clinician demonstrates the strategy with your child.
Try it with supportYou take a turn while the clinician coaches in the moment.
Use it all weekOne strategy, in one routine, until it is automatic.
04

Tracking progress

Progress in this age group is measurable, even when it is not yet words.

Vocabulary counts are useful and slow. Long before the word count moves, we can see growth in communication attempts per hour, in the range of reasons a child communicates, in imitation, in play complexity, and in how consistently they use the words they do have. Those are the early indicators that the system is coming online.
