Comprehensive speech-language evaluations

What a Comprehensive Speech-Language Evaluation Really Includes

A comprehensive evaluation is not one test, one score, or one hour. It is a carefully selected combination of interviews, observations, communication samples, clinical tasks, formal measures, and professional interpretation used to understand the whole child and determine responsible next steps.

Some evaluations are focused and finish in a single appointment. Others need additional time, specialized testing, device trials, a mealtime observation, or information from more than one environment. This page explains how we decide which, what happens at each stage, and what you are left holding at the end.

01What an evaluation is

An evaluation should provide an explanation, not just a score.

Norm-referenced standardized testing earns its place when the measure is appropriate for the child and administered and interpreted according to the test’s requirements. It compares performance with the test’s normative sample and gives families, schools, physicians, and other providers a shared reference point. What no single test or score can do is explain why a child communicates the way they do or determine the recommendation by itself. That explanation comes from reading results alongside interviews, observations, communication samples, dynamic assessment, and functional information. Whatever the referral concern, a speech-language evaluation is working toward four questions.

01

What is the child already doing successfully?

02

Where is communication, participation, learning, or mealtime becoming difficult?

03

What patterns or contributing factors may help explain those challenges?

04

What support, referral, monitoring, or follow-up, if any, would be appropriate?

“A score can tell you that something is different. It takes a full evaluation to tell you what to do about it.”

02How it works

The complete evaluation process

Once we know what the evaluation needs to answer, the work happens in three parts: the preparation before you arrive, the time with your child, and the analysis that follows. Families usually only see the middle one.

01

Before the appointment

The evaluation begins before we meet.

Whenever paperwork reaches us ahead of the visit, we read it before you arrive. Reviewing history and records in advance means the appointment can be spent watching your child communicate rather than collecting background we could have gathered beforehand.

Information may include

  • Developmental history
  • Medical history
  • Hearing history
  • Speech, language, communication, or feeding development
  • Previous evaluations
  • Previous or current therapy
  • School or childcare information
  • Diagnoses and current services
  • Family priorities
  • The child’s own concerns and goals
  • Languages and dialects used
  • Communication systems currently used

Relevant records may include

  • Previous speech-language evaluations
  • IEPs or 504 Plans
  • Audiology reports
  • Medical or developmental reports
  • AAC documentation
  • Feeding records
  • Therapy progress reports
  • Teacher or provider observations

Worth sendingSend whatever you already have, even if it is old or incomplete. A previous evaluation, a preschool progress note, or a short video on your phone can each answer something we would otherwise spend appointment time on. Please share records or videos only through the secure method we provide, rather than by regular email.

02

During the appointment

Comfortable for the child. Clinically intentional.

The evaluation may involve play, conversation, movement, storytelling, structured testing, speech practice, AAC use, or a familiar mealtime. The activities should be developmentally appropriate while still allowing the clinician to examine specific communication skills.

A child may

  • Begin with a preferred activity
  • Take breaks
  • Move around the room
  • Communicate through speech, gesture, sign, writing, or AAC
  • Demonstrate skills through play
  • Complete structured tasks when appropriate
  • Have a caregiver nearby when helpful
  • Warm up gradually before direct testing begins

“Child-friendly does not mean less thorough.”

A relaxed interaction may provide a more accurate communication sample. Structured tasks may then be added to examine particular skills, compare performance, test different supports, and clarify the clinical picture.

03

After the appointment

The clinical work continues after the session ends.

This is the part no family sees. After the visit, the clinician scores formal measures, reviews communication samples, compares performance across tasks, weighs the possible explanations against each other, develops recommendations, and writes the report.

  • Scoring and test interpretation
  • Speech or language sample analysis
  • Review of formal and informal findings
  • Analysis of functional impact
  • Differential diagnosis
  • Consideration of test limitations
  • Recommendations
  • Referrals
  • Report writing
  • Preparation for the results consultation

“Recommendations are not based on one score, one task, or one moment in the appointment. They are finalized after every relevant source of information has been considered together.”

Enough time to be accurate, without pushing past the child’s limits.

The direct-assessment portion is often scheduled for approximately one to two hours, but there is no single standard length and a longer session is not automatically a better one. What sets the time is how many areas need assessment, how your child is doing that day, and whether the information has to be gathered across more than one activity or setting.

Additional time or visits may be recommended when

  • The child needs time to become comfortable
  • Fatigue affects performance
  • Regulation affects participation
  • Several communication areas require assessment
  • Speech must be sampled across contexts
  • Motor speech testing requires repeated attempts
  • AAC systems must be trialed
  • A feeding evaluation requires different foods or routines
  • Provider or teacher information is still being collected
  • Testing is more accurate when divided into shorter sessions

Worth knowingIf a second visit would give us better information, we will say so when we schedule and explain exactly what it changes about the plan and the cost. Splitting an evaluation is a clinical decision, not an upsell.

03What we assess

The core components of a comprehensive evaluation

These are the building blocks. Not every evaluation uses all of them, and the ones we choose follow from the clinical question rather than a standard checklist. Here is what each one contributes.

01

Case history and record review

We review relevant developmental, medical, educational, communication, and treatment history. Previous reports may provide important context and help us avoid repeating unnecessary testing.

02

Caregiver and client interview

We discuss strengths, concerns, daily routines, communication environments, previous supports, and the questions the family wants answered. Older children should participate in this conversation whenever possible.

03

Clinical observation

We observe communication, interaction, regulation, play, problem-solving, participation, self-advocacy, and response to support.

04

Formal assessment

Norm-referenced, criterion-referenced, and other formal measures are used when they are appropriate, valid, and able to answer the clinical question. A measure may not be representative when its normative sample, language, administration requirements, response format, sensory or motor demands, or cultural assumptions do not fit the child. In those situations, a standard score may be misleading or may not be reportable. The report explains the limitation and gives appropriate weight to other valid information.

05

Informal and functional assessment

This may include play, conversation, storytelling, language sampling, reading or writing activities, real-world communication tasks, mealtime observation, and caregiver, teacher, or self-report measures.

06

Communication samples

Samples may be gathered through spontaneous communication, imitation, structured tasks, conversation, play, storytelling, reading, AAC, or recordings from everyday environments.

07

Dynamic assessment

Dynamic assessment examines how the child responds when the clinician teaches, models, cues, simplifies, or changes the task.

In plain termsWe do not only ask, “Can your child do this right now?” We also ask, “What helps them learn it?”

08

Oral mechanism examination

When clinically relevant, we complete a gentle examination of the structures and movements involved in speech or feeding. This may include the lips, tongue, jaw, palate, dentition, coordination, symmetry, and range of motion.

09

Hearing history and screening needs

Because hearing status can affect speech and language development, current hearing information is an important part of a comprehensive evaluation. We review the child’s hearing history and any recent hearing screening or audiology results. If current, age-appropriate hearing information is unavailable, or if the history, caregiver report, or evaluation raises concern, we recommend hearing screening or a comprehensive audiologic evaluation through an appropriate provider. Social House Therapy does not provide comprehensive audiologic evaluations.

10

Functional impact

We consider how communication affects being understood, expressing needs and ideas, following directions, participating at school, building relationships, completing routines, advocating for oneself, and participating in meals.

11

Differential diagnosis

Many communication concerns can look similar on the surface, and more than one condition or influence can be present at the same time. We analyze the child’s complete profile to determine which explanation, or which combination of explanations, best fits the available information.

12

Referrals and collaboration

When appropriate, recommendations may include audiology, ENT, developmental pediatrics, occupational therapy, physical therapy, psychology, nutrition, gastroenterology, dentistry, school-based evaluation, or another specialist.

Questions we differentiate, and factors that can occur together

Identifying that a child is having difficulty is only the beginning. A comprehensive evaluation examines the child’s communication profile, functional impact, and possible contributing factors. It asks whether the findings reflect expected developmental or community variation, a language or dialect difference, a communication disorder, another influence, or a combination. These are not always either/or questions, and more than one area may need consideration at once.

Not every evaluation will result in a definitive diagnosis. When findings remain unclear, the report should explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and what additional information may be helpful.

Communication must be understood within the child’s language, culture, and community.

A responsible evaluation considers every language and dialect the child uses, where and with whom each is used, the length and type of exposure, and the cultural expectations that shape interaction. Assessment should include the languages and communication modes needed to answer the referral question to the greatest extent feasible, using a qualified multilingual clinician or trained interpreter when appropriate.

  • Languages and dialects used
  • Where and with whom each language is used
  • Length and type of exposure
  • Cultural communication expectations
  • Interpreter support when needed
  • Whether testing norms represent the child
  • Language difference versus language disorder
  • Dynamic assessment
  • Descriptive assessment
  • Limitations of standardized scores

“Speaking more than one language does not cause a language disorder.”

Standard scores are reported only when the normative sample and administration procedures support valid interpretation. When a measure is translated, modified beyond permitted procedures, or otherwise not representative, the report documents those limitations instead of presenting the score as a valid standardized comparison, and greater weight is given to descriptive assessment, language sampling, caregiver and teacher report, dynamic assessment, and functional observation.

03

Childhood Apraxia and Motor Speech

Suspected childhood apraxia or another motor speech disorder often requires assessment beyond a traditional articulation test.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Consistency across repeated attempts
  • Vowel accuracy
  • Sound transitions
  • Motor planning
  • Sequencing
  • Multisyllabic words
  • Rate
  • Rhythm
  • Stress
  • Prosody
  • Oral structures
  • Coordination
  • Response to different cueing methods
  • Overall intelligibility

Why it mattersMotor speech assessment often requires repeated trials, dynamic cueing, and close analysis of how speech changes with support.

Research has not validated any single feature, or list of features, that reliably separates childhood apraxia of speech from other speech sound disorders, so it is assessed by an SLP with pediatric motor speech training who weighs the complete speech profile against phonological, articulatory, structural, and other explanations. For very young children or children with limited speech, a provisional impression with continued assessment over time is often more appropriate than a definitive label on day one.

AAC

AAC assessment involves matching communication supports to the individual rather than selecting a device based on diagnosis alone. There are no prerequisite skills a child must demonstrate before AAC can be considered. AAC may supplement speech, support speech development, or provide another reliable way to communicate.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Current communication methods
  • Communication functions
  • Receptive and expressive language
  • Symbol understanding
  • Visual access
  • Motor access
  • Positioning
  • Vocabulary organization
  • Communication partners
  • Low-tech systems
  • High-tech systems
  • Device or system trials
  • Feature matching
  • Training needs
  • Implementation needs
  • Documentation needs

Why it mattersWe are not searching for one universally best device. We are identifying a flexible, often multimodal, communication system whose features fit the person across activities, environments, and communication partners.

08

Feeding, Mealtime, and Swallowing

A pediatric feeding and swallowing evaluation considers the whole feeding experience: medical and developmental history, nutrition and hydration, oral and swallowing skills, sensory responses, accepted foods and liquids, positioning and equipment, mealtime routines, caregiver concerns, and the child’s participation and comfort. Difficulties may involve one or more medical, nutritional, feeding-skill, sensory, swallowing, and psychosocial factors, and collaboration with the child’s medical team may be needed.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Medical and feeding history
  • Growth, nutrition, and hydration concerns
  • Gastrointestinal and respiratory history
  • Foods and liquids accepted
  • Diet variety
  • Sensory responses
  • Food acceptance
  • Mealtime length
  • Positioning
  • Cups, utensils, bottles, and straws
  • Lip, tongue, and jaw movement
  • Oral structures
  • Chewing
  • Lateralization
  • Drinking skills
  • Coughing
  • Choking
  • Congestion
  • Wet vocal quality
  • Fatigue, efficiency, and endurance
  • Whether nutrition and hydration needs can be met orally
  • Family participation
  • Mealtime stress
  • Need for additional medical assessment

Why it mattersWe look at the child, the food, the environment, and the family’s experience of mealtimes.

Safety note. A clinical feeding and swallowing evaluation can identify signs of difficulty and help determine next steps, but it cannot directly visualize pharyngeal swallowing anatomy and physiology or rule out aspiration in every child. Research reviews show that aspiration in young children is frequently silent, producing no cough or outward sign at the moment it happens. When more information about swallowing safety is needed, medical follow-up and an instrumental swallowing evaluation, such as a videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES), may be recommended.

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a mental health diagnosis in the DSM-5, and diagnosing it is outside an SLP’s scope. Its care is interdisciplinary: we may recognize possible signs, refer for the mental health and medical evaluation the diagnosis requires, and assess and treat co-occurring feeding-skill or swallowing difficulties as part of that team.

06Using the results

Documentation that can support the next conversation

An evaluation is most useful when it does not sit in a drawer. With your written permission, the report can go to the people already supporting your child, so that everyone is working from the same information instead of separate impressions.

Your evaluation report

Pediatricians School and IEP teams Preschools or childcare providers Other speech-language pathologists Occupational therapists Physical therapists Psychologists Audiologists ENT physicians Dietitians Feeding teams ABA providers AAC vendors Funding sources Insurance or benefit programs when applicable

What the report gives you is clinical documentation and specific recommendations to bring to those conversations. What each organization does with it is their decision. Schools, medical providers, insurers, funding sources, and government programs each apply their own criteria, so a private evaluation cannot guarantee eligibility, authorization, reimbursement, equipment funding, or school-based services.

Sharing with a school. Many families bring the report to an IEP or 504 meeting. When a parent shares an outside evaluation that meets the public agency’s applicable criteria, IDEA requires the agency to consider its results in decisions about the child’s free appropriate public education (34 CFR 300.502(c)). Consideration does not mean the school must adopt every conclusion or recommendation. The school remains responsible for its own eligibility, IEP, service, and placement decisions, and having outside documentation in hand often makes that conversation more specific. This is general educational information, not legal advice.

07Why Social House Therapy

Clinical depth without losing sight of the child

01
Built around the clinical question

Every evaluation starts from your referral concern, not from a standing list of tests. Two children the same age may be assessed very differently, and that is the point.

02
Formal and functional read together

Scores are interpreted next to observation, communication samples, history, and what you tell us. When those sources disagree, we investigate the disagreement instead of defaulting to the number.

03
Children are helped to show what they can do

Your child gets time to warm up and can demonstrate skills through play, movement, and preferred activities. A comfortable child gives us more accurate information than a compliant one.

04
We look for the pattern, not just the result

Two children can miss the same test items for completely different reasons, and the difference changes the plan. The evaluation examines which interpretation best fits the available evidence.

05
Recommendations you can actually use

Suggestions are written for the places your child really communicates: home routines, the classroom, the dinner table, time with grandparents. If it cannot be used on an ordinary Tuesday, it does not belong in the report.

06
Your priorities shape the plan

What matters to your family becomes part of the clinical record, and older children are asked directly what they want to be able to do.

07
Findings and limits explained plainly

We tell you what each score means, where a measure was a poor fit, and what is still uncertain. You should finish knowing the limits of what we found, not only the headline.

08
You talk to the clinician who was there

The person who evaluated your child writes the report and normally reviews it with you, so you are talking with someone who watched your child rather than someone reading notes second-hand.

It is not medical advice, it does not replace an individualized clinical assessment, and reading it does not create a provider-patient relationship with Social House Therapy. What any particular evaluation includes is determined by the treating clinician after reviewing your child’s history and concerns.

Scope. Social House Therapy provides evaluations in the areas described on this page when they fall within the clinician’s scope and competence.
