Comprehensive speech-language evaluations

What a Comprehensive Speech-Language Evaluation Really Includes

A comprehensive evaluation is not one test, one score, or one hour. It is a carefully selected combination of formal and informal measures, like standardized testing, observations, interviews, communication samples, and oral mechanism examinations, considered together with the case history and brought together through professional interpretation to understand the whole child and determine responsible next steps.

Every evaluation is completed in a single appointment. We will never ask you to schedule additional follow-up evaluation time: your intake paperwork is reviewed in advance, so follow-up questions from the case history are ready before we meet, and the session is planned so everything your child needs, even specialized testing, fits inside one visit. This page explains how that planning works, what happens at each stage, and what you are left holding at the end.

01What an evaluation is

An evaluation should provide an explanation, not just a score.

Every child is different, so every evaluation looks a little different. Depending on your child’s age, communication needs, and the reason for the evaluation, we develop a customized testing battery. Standardized tests can be helpful for comparing certain skills with same-age peers, and they give families, schools, physicians, and other providers a shared reference point. But they are not always needed or the best fit for every child, and no single test or score can tell the whole story. Our job is to bring all of the information together to understand your child’s strengths, identify areas where they may need support, and give you clear recommendations for what comes next. Every evaluation is designed to answer four important questions.

01

What is the child already doing successfully?

02

Where is communication, participation, learning, or mealtime becoming difficult?

03

What might be making those things harder for your child?

04

What support, referral, monitoring, or follow-up, if any, would be appropriate?

The evaluation is designed around the question we need to answer.

This is the step families rarely see. Before any material is chosen, your concern becomes a specific clinical question, and the question determines the method. A child who is hard to understand needs a different assessment than a child who stutters, uses AAC, struggles to follow directions, or has a hard time at meals. Some children need a close look at one area. Others need a broader picture across several.

02How it works

The complete evaluation process

Once we know what the evaluation needs to answer, the work happens in three parts: the preparation before the evaluation, the time with your child, and the analysis that follows. That holds wherever we meet, whether we come to your home or your child comes to us. Families usually only see the middle piece.

01

Before the appointment

The evaluation begins before we meet.

Whenever paperwork reaches us ahead of the visit, we read it before the evaluation begins. Reviewing history and records in advance means the appointment can be spent watching your child communicate rather than collecting background we could have gathered beforehand.

Information may include

  • Developmental and birth history
  • Medical history, diagnosis, and medications
  • Hearing & vision history
  • Speech, language, communication, or feeding development
  • Previous or current therapies and services
  • School or childcare information
  • Family priorities
  • The child’s own concerns and goals
  • Languages and dialects used
  • Communication methods or systems currently used
  • Daily routines and environments where concerns are most noticeable

Relevant records may include

  • Previous speech-language, feeding, or related evaluations
  • IEPs or 504 Plans
  • Audiology reports
  • Medical or developmental reports
  • AAC evaluations or documentation
  • Feeding and swallowing records
  • Therapy progress reports
  • Therapist, teacher, or provider observations

Worth sendingSend whatever you already have, even if it is old or incomplete. A previous evaluation, a preschool progress note, or a short video on your phone can each answer something we would otherwise spend appointment time on. Please share records or videos only through the secure method we provide, rather than by regular email.

02

During the appointment

Comfortable for the child. Clinically intentional.

The evaluation may involve play, conversation, movement, storytelling, structured testing, AAC use, or a familiar mealtime. Part of the session is simply observation without prompting, so we can see what your child does independently. Part is dynamic assessment: practicing or repeating sounds and words with prompting to find which level of support helps your child make progress. The activities should be developmentally appropriate while still allowing the clinician to examine specific communication skills.

A child may

  • Begin with a preferred activity
  • Take breaks
  • Move around the room
  • Communicate through speech, gesture, sign, vocalizations, writing, or AAC
  • Demonstrate skills through play
  • Complete structured tasks when appropriate
  • Have a caregiver nearby when helpful
  • Warm up gradually before direct testing begins

“Child-friendly does not mean less thorough.”

A relaxed interaction may provide a more accurate communication sample. Structured tasks may then be added to examine particular skills, compare performance, test different supports, and clarify the clinical picture.

03

After the appointment

The clinical work continues after the session ends.

This is the part no family sees. After the visit, the clinician scores the formal and informal measures and analyzes the communication samples: counting the sounds your child uses and how accurately, and looking at vocabulary, word count, and grammar. Skills are weighed against what is expected at your child’s age to understand whether they are developing as expected or may benefit from additional support. Those results are then interpreted together with the history, risk factors, and what you reported as a parent, weighing the possible explanations against each other before developing recommendations and writing the report.

  • Scoring and test interpretation
  • Review of formal and informal findings
  • Integration of results, observations, history, records, and caregiver input
  • Clinical interpretation and diagnostic considerations
  • Analysis of speech, language, communication, or feeding samples, when appropriate
  • Analysis of functional impact
  • Consideration of test limitations
  • Recommendations
  • Referrals, when appropriate
  • Individualized treatment planning and goal development, when appropriate
  • Report writing
  • Preparation for the results consultation

Enough time to be accurate, without pushing past the child’s limits.

The direct-assessment portion typically runs about an hour, and a longer session is not automatically a better one: about an hour with your child is usually enough to see the big picture. Much of the evaluation’s work happens afterward, in scoring, analysis, and interpretation. We set the length when we schedule, so everything is completed in one visit.

03What we assess

The core components of a comprehensive evaluation

These are the building blocks. Not every evaluation uses all of them, and the ones we choose follow from the clinical question rather than a standard checklist. Here is what each one contributes.

01

Case history and record review

We review relevant developmental, medical, educational, communication, and treatment history. Previous reports may provide important context, help us avoid repeating unnecessary testing, and allow us to measure change over time: whether skills are growing quickly, growing slowly, holding steady, or have been lost.

02

Caregiver and client interview

We discuss strengths, concerns, daily routines, communication environments, previous supports, and the questions the family wants answered. Older children should participate in this conversation whenever possible.

03

Hearing and vision history

Hearing plays a direct role in how speech and language develop, and vision affects how a child accesses pictures, books, gestures, AAC, and many evaluation activities. So we review your child’s hearing and vision history, any previous screening results, and anything you have noticed or wondered about. If current information is unavailable, or if something during the evaluation raises a question, we may recommend a screening or evaluation with an audiologist, optometrist, ophthalmologist, or other appropriate provider. Social House Therapy does not provide comprehensive hearing or vision evaluations.

04

Clinical observation

Clinical observation happens throughout the evaluation, not just at one point. We watch how your child communicates, interacts, plays, problem-solves, regulates, participates, and moves through their environment, and how they respond to different people, activities, and levels of support. We also notice what happens when a message is misunderstood, and how your child and their communication partner work together to repair it. These observations help us understand how skills show up in real moments, beyond individual test items.

05

Formal assessment

Standardized tests and other formal measures are used when they are appropriate and useful for answering the clinical question. Sometimes a test is not a fair fit for a child: the children in the test’s comparison group, its language, its instructions, its response format, or its sensory or motor demands may not match how your child communicates. In those situations, a standard score may not be appropriate to report or interpret, so the report explains why and relies on the other information gathered instead. An evaluation without a standard score is not less comprehensive; it means the score would not have given a fair or meaningful picture of your child’s abilities.

06

Informal and functional assessment

Informal assessment includes nonstandardized ways of gathering information about your child’s skills. These measures do not rely on the fixed testing procedures or comparison scores of standardized tests, but they can provide equally important clinical information. Depending on the questions being asked, this may include play-based activities, clinician-created tasks or probes, storytelling, reading or writing activities, stimulability testing (checking whether a new sound or skill emerges with a little help), caregiver or teacher questionnaires, mealtime observation, and real-world communication tasks.

07

Communication samples

A communication sample is a foundational part of every evaluation we complete. It lets us see how your child communicates beyond individual test questions, in situations that look much more like everyday life. Samples may be gathered through conversation, play, storytelling, reading, structured activities, AAC use, or recordings from everyday environments. What we analyze depends on your child’s communication needs and the questions we are trying to answer. For example:

  • Speech: how understandable your child is in connected speech, which sounds are difficult, whether error patterns are consistent, and how speech changes as words and sentences become more complex.
  • Fluency: the types and frequency of stuttering, moments of tension or struggle, and behaviors such as changing words, avoiding speaking, or trying to escape a stutter.
  • Language: vocabulary, grammar, sentence length and complexity, storytelling, organization of ideas, and how clearly your child expresses a message. We can also observe how they understand questions, directions, and conversation in context.
  • Social communication: how your child initiates and responds, takes turns, maintains or shifts topics, interprets and uses verbal and nonverbal information, and repairs communication when something is misunderstood.

Because these samples reflect communication in context, they can reveal strengths, patterns, and challenges that may not appear during standardized testing, and they provide a meaningful baseline for measuring progress over time.

08

Dynamic assessment

Dynamic assessment looks at how a child responds to teaching and support. Rather than measuring only what the child can do independently, the clinician may provide models, cues, or practice to see how the child learns and what helps them be successful. This can help us tell the difference between a skill your child simply has not learned yet and a skill that stays difficult even with support, which matters for both diagnosis and planning therapy that actually helps.

09

Oral mechanism examination

When clinically relevant, we complete a gentle examination of the structures and movements of the mouth and face that support speech, feeding, breathing, and swallowing. The clinician may assess areas such as lip and tongue movement, jaw control, oral posture, palate structure, breathing patterns, and coordination. This helps determine whether structural or movement-related factors may be contributing to speech, feeding, or oral-function concerns. The depth of the assessment depends on the child’s needs and the reason for the evaluation.

10

Functional impact

We consider how communication affects being understood, expressing needs and ideas, following directions, participating at school, building relationships, completing routines, advocating for oneself, and participating in meals.

11

Clinical interpretation and differential diagnosis

Many communication concerns can look similar on the surface, and more than one condition or influence can be present at the same time. We analyze the child’s complete profile to determine which explanation, or which combination of explanations, best fits the available information.

12

Referrals and collaboration

When appropriate, recommendations may include audiology, ENT, developmental pediatrics, occupational therapy, physical therapy, psychology, nutrition, gastroenterology, dentistry, school-based evaluation, or another specialist.

Questions we differentiate, and factors that can occur together

Identifying that a child is having difficulty is only the beginning. A comprehensive evaluation examines the child’s communication profile, functional impact, and possible contributing factors. It asks whether the findings reflect expected developmental or community variation, a language or dialect difference, a communication disorder, another influence, or a combination. These are not always either/or questions, and more than one area may need consideration at once.

Not every evaluation will result in a definitive diagnosis. When findings remain unclear, the report should explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and what additional information may be helpful.

Communication must be understood within the child’s language, culture, and community.

A responsible evaluation considers every language and dialect a child uses, where and with whom each is used, the length and type of exposure, and the cultural expectations that shape communication. Social House Therapy does not have a bilingual clinician at this time, so formal, standardized testing is completed in English. Your child’s full language experience is still part of the evaluation: we gather information about the other languages spoken in your home through caregiver interview, records, communication samples, and dynamic assessment, and the report states which language testing was completed in and what that means for how the results should be read. When answering the clinical question requires direct assessment in another language, we say so and refer you to a clinician who can provide it, and we discuss interpreter support when it is clinically appropriate. The goal is to distinguish a language or dialect difference from a true communication disorder, without treating multilingual development as a deficit.

  • Languages and dialects used at home and in the community
  • Where and with whom each language is used
  • Length and type of exposure
  • Cultural communication expectations
  • The language formal testing was completed in, stated in the report
  • Informal information about the child’s other languages
  • Whether testing norms represent the child
  • Language difference versus language disorder
  • Dynamic and descriptive assessment
  • Limitations of standardized scores
  • Interpreter support, or referral, when direct testing in another language is required

“Speaking more than one language does not cause a language disorder.”

Test scores compare your child to a specific group of children, tested in one language and in a specific way. If your child was not well represented in that group, a standard score may not be a valid comparison and may not be appropriate to report at all. When that is the case we say so, and rely more heavily on communication samples, caregiver interview, dynamic assessment, observation, and descriptive findings to understand how your child communicates across everyday settings.

04Specialized evaluations

What we assess based on the area of concern

The methods depend on the area of concern and the questions the evaluation needs to answer. Open any area to see what it examines and why those findings matter.

01

Early Communication and Late Talking

For young children, communication includes much more than spoken vocabulary. An evaluation looks at how your child understands language, connects with others, plays, and communicates using sounds, gestures, words, signs, AAC, and other forms of communication.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Understanding words, questions, and directions
  • Vocabulary
  • Word combinations
  • Early grammar
  • Reasons for communicating, such as requesting, protesting, sharing, or getting attention
  • Sharing attention with another person
  • Speech sound development
  • Spoken words
  • Gestures
  • Signs
  • AAC or other communication supports
  • Imitation
  • Play development
  • Communication during familiar routines
  • Hearing history and oral-motor skills when relevant

Why it mattersChildren can use few spoken words for many different reasons. We look at the whole communication profile to understand what your child already does well, where development may be more difficult, and whether the pattern points primarily to language, speech production, hearing, another developmental factor, or a combination. This helps us determine whether support is needed and where it should begin.

Learn about late talkers and early communication.

02

Speech Sound Disorders

A speech sound evaluation looks at more than which sounds a child says correctly or incorrectly. We examine how speech sounds are produced, whether there are patterns in the errors, and how those differences affect how easily your child is understood in everyday conversation.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Speech sounds in single words
  • Speech sounds in spontaneous conversation
  • How easily your child is understood
  • Patterns in how sounds are changed or simplified
  • Whether errors are consistent
  • Whether sounds become easier with a model or cue
  • How sounds are sequenced in longer or more complex words
  • Awareness of sounds in words and early reading skills, when age-appropriate
  • Oral structures and movement when relevant
  • Effects on participation, learning, or confidence

Why it mattersTwo children may make similar speech errors for very different reasons. Looking at the full pattern helps us understand whether the difficulty is mainly about producing individual sounds, organizing sound patterns, motor planning and coordination, structural factors, or a combination, and that distinction guides where treatment should begin.

Explore speech sound evaluations and therapy.

03

Childhood Apraxia and Motor Speech

Suspected childhood apraxia or another motor speech disorder often requires assessment beyond a traditional articulation test.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Consistency across repeated attempts
  • Vowel accuracy
  • How smoothly sounds and syllables move together
  • Planning and sequencing speech movements
  • Longer or multisyllabic words
  • Speech rate, rhythm, and stress
  • Coordination of speech movements
  • Oral structures and movement when relevant
  • How speech changes with different cues or supports
  • Overall how easily your child is understood

Why it mattersMotor speech difficulties can look similar to other speech sound disorders. We look at the full speech pattern, including how consistent errors are, how speech movements are coordinated, and how your child responds to different types of support. For very young children or children with limited speech, the clearest answer may develop over time rather than from one appointment.

See how motor speech assessment differs from articulation testing.

04

Language

Language is how we understand and use words, sentences, and ideas. An evaluation looks at how your child understands what others say, expresses their own thoughts, organizes information, tells stories, and uses language in everyday life.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Understanding words, questions, and directions
  • Vocabulary and word meanings
  • Grammar and sentence development
  • Word retrieval
  • Organizing thoughts and expressing ideas clearly
  • Sequencing events and telling stories in a logical order
  • Understanding concepts and relationships between words
  • Making inferences
  • Understanding nonliteral or more complex language
  • Language used for reading, writing, and learning, when age-appropriate
  • How language skills affect everyday participation

Why it mattersA child can pass a vocabulary test and still lose the thread of a story, a set of directions, or a classroom discussion. Separating what your child understands from what they can organize and express tells us where to start, because the two need very different kinds of support.

Explore receptive, expressive, and social language support.

05

Social Communication

Social communication is how we use communication to connect, participate, and navigate interactions with other people. An evaluation looks at how your child participates in conversations, communicates across different people and situations, notices and responds to social information, and handles moments when communication breaks down.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Starting and maintaining interactions
  • Conversation and topic management
  • Repairing misunderstandings
  • Understanding implied meaning
  • Reading tone and body language
  • Understanding sarcasm and figurative language
  • Communication across people and settings
  • Self-advocacy and communication preferences
  • Environmental supports and barriers

Why it mattersThere is no single correct way to communicate, so we are not measuring your child against a script. We are looking for where interactions break down, what already helps them go well, and which supports would make them feel less effortful.

Explore social communication support.

06

Stuttering and Fluency

Fluency is about more than how speech sounds to a listener. A fluency evaluation focuses on the speaker’s experience: how speaking feels, how stuttering affects participation, confidence, and communication choices, and which situations make speaking easier or harder. We also look at the communication environment to understand what helps the speaker feel supported, what may increase pressure, and how communication partners can make speaking feel more comfortable.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Types and frequency of stuttering
  • Physical tension or struggle
  • Thoughts and feelings about speaking
  • Escape or avoidant behaviors
  • Impact on participation and confidence
  • Speaking across different situations
  • Speech rate and rhythm
  • Communication partner responses
  • Family history
  • Self-advocacy

Why it mattersThe goal is not simply to count stutters. We want to understand how stuttering affects the speaker’s daily life and what support may help them communicate more freely and confidently.

Learn about stuttering evaluation and support.

07

Feeding, Mealtime, and Swallowing

Feeding involves much more than what foods a child will eat. An evaluation looks at how your child eats and drinks, the skills their body uses during meals, their sensory responses, comfort and safety, and how feeding affects the child and family. Medical, developmental, sensory, feeding-skill, and environmental factors may all play a role.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Medical and feeding history
  • Growth, nutrition, and hydration concerns
  • Foods and liquids currently accepted
  • Food variety and sensory responses
  • Mealtime routines and participation
  • Positioning and feeding equipment
  • Oral structures and movement
  • Biting, chewing, and tongue movement
  • Cup, straw, and bottle drinking
  • Swallowing signs and symptoms
  • Feeding efficiency and endurance
  • Comfort and stress during meals
  • Caregiver concerns and priorities
  • Need for additional medical assessment

Why it mattersFeeding difficulties rarely come down to one thing. We look at the child, the food, the environment, and the family’s experience of mealtimes to understand what may be making eating or drinking difficult and what kind of support may help.

Safety note. A clinical feeding and swallowing evaluation can identify signs that additional swallowing or medical assessment may be needed, but it cannot directly see what happens inside the throat during swallowing. When swallowing safety remains a concern, an instrumental swallowing study or medical referral may be recommended.

Learn about pediatric feeding and mealtime support.

08

Cognitive-Communication and Executive Function

Cognitive-communication skills are the thinking skills we use to understand, organize, remember, and communicate information. An evaluation looks at how areas such as attention, memory, planning, organization, and problem-solving affect communication, learning, and everyday participation. This type of evaluation focuses on how cognitive skills affect communication; when broader concerns about learning, attention, memory, or cognition need to be evaluated, we may recommend assessment by another appropriate professional.

What the evaluation may examine

  • Attention and focus
  • Working memory
  • Processing information
  • Organizing thoughts and tasks
  • Planning and sequencing
  • Starting and completing tasks
  • Problem-solving
  • Flexible thinking
  • Self-monitoring
  • Following complex directions
  • Managing everyday communication tasks
  • Academic or workplace participation
  • Self-advocacy
  • Environmental supports

Why it mattersA person may know what they want to say but have difficulty keeping track of information, organizing their thoughts, getting started, or managing several steps at once. Understanding how these thinking skills affect communication helps us identify useful strategies and supports.

Recognize your child in one of these?

You do not need to be certain, and you do not need to land on the right category. Working out which evaluation your child needs is our job, not yours. Tell us which one sounded closest and we will take it from there.
