# Stuttering & Fluency page: accuracy review (August 2026)

Reviewed against the ASHA Practice Portal (Fluency Disorders) and primary research. Claim-by-claim verdicts; page updated same day. Same process as the AAC, autism, speech-sound, and language reviews.

## Verified and kept
1. **"8% of children ever stutter; estimates range 5-11%."** Yairi & Ambrose (2013), J Fluency Disorders 38(2):66-87: lifetime incidence ~8% or higher (older estimates ~5%); Reilly et al. (2013), Pediatrics 132(3):460-467: 11.2% cumulative onset by age 4 in a community cohort. The page's range spans exactly these sources. Kept.
2. **"1% of adults stutter; most childhood stuttering resolves, but not all."** Yairi & Ambrose 2013 (~1% adult prevalence); recovery rates ~65-80% (Yairi & Ambrose 1999: persistency/recovery cohort; Reilly cohort similar). Kept.
3. **Onset most common between ages 2 and 4.** Yairi & Ambrose data; consistent with ASHA portal. Kept.
4. **Neurological + genetic causation; not anxiety, parenting, trauma, bilingualism.** Smith & Weber (2017) multifactorial dynamic pathways theory (cited); Frigerio-Domingues & Drayna (2017) genetics (cited); consistent with ASHA portal etiology section. The anxiety causal-arrow framing (consequence, not cause) matches the literature. Kept.
5. **Typical vs stuttering-like disfluency table.** Sound/syllable repetitions, prolongations, blocks vs whole-word/phrase repetitions and revisions: standard SLD vs other-disfluency distinction from the Illinois studies (Yairi & Ambrose) and ASHA portal. Kept.
6. **Risk factors for persistence** (6+ months duration, family history of persistence, later onset after ~3.5, tension/struggle, awareness, co-occurring speech-language concerns): matches Yairi & Ambrose risk-factor literature and Stuttering Foundation guidance (both cited). Kept.
7. **Preschool intervention effective; parent-implemented.** Jones et al. (2005) BMJ RCT (Lidcombe, cited); de Sonneville-Koedoot et al. (2015) RESTART trial (cited): direct and indirect approaches both produced high recovery (~76-77%). Page describes both program families without brand promises. Kept.
8. **"Talking about stuttering does not make it worse."** The diagnosogenic-era belief is not supported; openness/desensitization is standard in current practice (ASHA portal). Kept.
9. **Bullying risk.** Blood & Blood (2004), cited. Kept.
10. **Cluttering description** (rapid/irregular rate, collapsed syllables, less tension, can co-occur): matches ASHA portal. Kept.
11. **"No medication approved for stuttering."** Re-verified as of August 2026: ecopipam completed Phase 2 in adults (Speak Freely, NCT04492956); Teva's June 2026 NDA for ecopipam is for pediatric Tourette syndrome, not stuttering; drugs.com (July 2026): FDA approved: No. FAQ now says "as of this review" and notes drugs are in trials, so the page dates itself honestly.

## Honest limits (kept visible on page)
- Recovery is common but individually unpredictable; the page says "nobody can tell you reliably whether your child will" rather than quoting a recovery percentage as a promise.
- Devices: "help some adults somewhat" reflects the mixed altered-auditory-feedback evidence; no endorsement.
- Older-child progress and "families report the pause makes the biggest difference" claims are clinical observations, framed as such rather than as statistics.
- August 2026: page reframed to the practice's served age range (2 to 12); teen-specific framing removed from audience-facing copy while research citations (adult prevalence, adolescent bullying study) remain factual context.
