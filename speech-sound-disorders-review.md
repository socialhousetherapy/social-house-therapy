# Speech Sound Disorders page: accuracy review

## September 2026 pass: deep research, parent audits, readability

Second full pass. Every August verdict below was rechecked and stands. New sources verified against the publisher record before citing.

### New evidence added (all verified this pass)
1. **CAS prevalence and diagnostic honesty.** Shriberg, Kwiatkowski & Mabie (2019), Clinical Linguistics & Phonetics 33(8):679-706, via the ASHA CAS portal: point prevalence about 1 in 1,000 children aged 4 to 8; about 2.4% of children with idiopathic speech delay. Murray, Iuzzini-Seigel, Maas, Terband & Ballard (2021), AJSLP 30(1):279-300: no single validated diagnostic feature differentiates CAS; diagnosis rests on consensus features across tasks. Page now gives the number and states plainly that there is no single test.
2. **Dose frequency.** Allen (2013), JSLHR 56(3):865-877, randomized trial of 54 preschoolers: multiple oppositions three times a week for 8 weeks outperformed once a week for 24 weeks with total sessions held constant, and the once-weekly group did not differ significantly from a storybook control. Added to a new FAQ ("How often will we need to come?") and a new value ("Frequency is set by the evidence, not the calendar"). Stated as one trial of one approach, not a universal rule.
3. **Parents vs clinicians.** Law, Garrett & Nye (2004), JSLHR 47(4):924-943, meta-analysis: therapy effective for phonological difficulties; no significant difference between interventions delivered by trained parents and by clinicians; longer duration associated with better outcomes. Used to back "home practice is not a nice-to-have" and the coached-practice model.
4. **Literacy risk made precise.** Hayiou-Thomas, Carroll, Leavett, Hulme & Snowling (2017), JCPP 58(2):197-205: early SSD alone conferred a small but significant risk; persistence to school entry, atypical ("disordered") errors, and co-occurring language impairment raised it substantially. The August page said "higher risk" without qualification; it now says where the risk concentrates, which also justifies assessing language alongside sounds.
5. **Persistence at age eight.** Wren, Miller, Peters, Emond & Roulstone (2016), JSLHR 59(4):647-673, ALSPAC: persistent SSD at 8 was 3.6%, excluding common residual distortions. Used in the therapy intro to say honestly that most children get there and therapy speeds and completes the process.
6. **Tongue tie.** AAP clinical report (Thomas et al., 2024), Pediatrics 154(2):e2024067605, added beside Chinnadurai (2015): frenotomy to prevent speech problems is not evidence-based.
7. **Ear infections softened.** Roberts, Rosenfeld & Zeisel (2004) and Wren (2016) both find otitis media with effusion is not a meaningful predictor of SSD. The hearing aside and the signs list no longer imply that infections explain speech errors; a current hearing check is still recommended.

# August 2026 review (retained)

Reviewed against the ASHA Practice Portal (Speech Sound Disorders; CAS Technical Report) and primary research. Claim-by-claim verdicts; page updated same day. Same process as aac-review.md and autism-social-communication-review.md.

## Verified and kept
1. **Intelligibility milestones (the page's headline stats).** Hustad, Mahr, Natzke & Rathouz (2021), JSLHR 64(10):3707-3719. Confirmed exactly: 538 typically developing children, 1,076 naive adult listeners transcribing recordings; 50th-percentile single-word intelligibility reached 50% at 2;7 (31 mo) and 75% at 4;1 (49 mo), 90% at 6;11; lower-percentile children keep growing past age 9 (90% single-word range 83 to 120+ months). Counters, body copy, FAQ, and schema all match the study. Kept unchanged.
2. **The old "100% by four" rule was parent-estimate based and optimistic.** Coplan & Gleason (1988) formula (age/4 x 100); Flipsen (2006) noted it was never validated against objective measures; Hustad 2021 is the corrective. Page framing verified.
3. **66% threshold at age four.** Gordon-Brannan & Hodson (2000), AJSLP 9(2):141-150: children under ~66% intelligible to unfamiliar listeners at age 4 are candidates for intervention. Kept.
4. **"At least 50% intelligible by four."** Consistent with Hustad 2021's lower-percentile benchmarks and how the study's authors and clinical commentators state the referral floor. Kept.
5. **Phonological process elimination ages** (FCD ~3;3, fronting ~3;6, stopping 3-5, cluster reduction ~4, weak syllable deletion ~4, gliding ~5). Match Bowen's synthesis table (cited), which draws on Grunwell (1997). Page now adds the honest caveat that textbooks vary by a few months (ranges, not cliffs).
6. **Oral motor exercises do not improve speech.** McCauley et al. (2009) ASHA-commissioned systematic review: no evidence base; Lof & Watson (2008) survey documents continued use anyway. Kept, with one new joke at the expense of bubbles.
7. **CAS hallmark features and "different, more intensive therapy."** Match the ASHA (2007) CAS Technical Report's three consensus features (inconsistent errors, disrupted transitions, atypical prosody) plus commonly listed clinical signs. Added Murray, McCabe & Ballard (2014) AJSLP systematic review to sources as the treatment-outcomes evidence (motor-based approaches, high dose).
8. **Approach descriptions** (minimal pairs, cycles, complexity, core vocabulary) are consistent with Baker & McLeod (2011) and the ASHA portal. Kept.
9. **Speech-literacy link.** Well supported; added a primary source, Peterson, Pennington, Shriberg & Boada (2009), JSLHR 52(5):1175-1188 (children with SSD, especially with language impairment, at elevated literacy risk).
10. **School eligibility vs need, difference-not-disorder, telehealth suitability, hearing checks.** All consistent with ASHA portal positions (eligibility criteria are narrower than clinical need; dialect differences are not disorders; audiology referral).

## Fixed
1. **Em dashes (7 on-page instances)** violating the project rule: six em-dash entities in the phonological-pattern accordion titles and one pair in the "unusual is more concerning" callout, plus two literal em dashes in ASHA reference titles. All replaced with colons/parentheses/commas.
2. **Sound-age groupings ran about a year late vs current norms.** Crowe & McLeod (2020), AJSLP 29(4) (15 studies, n=18,907, 90% criterion): plosives/nasals/glides by 3;11, so /t/ moved from the "by 3" list to the "by 4" list; s, z, v, sh, ch, j, l are acquired 4;0-4;11, so "By ages 5 to 6" retitled "By age 5"; r/th/zh acquired 5;0-6;11, so "Ages 6 to 7" retitled "Ages 5 to 7" with r landing around six. This also makes the section internally consistent with its own "mastered earlier than older charts implied" sentence.
3. **"R is the most common single reason school-age children are referred"**: not citable as stated; softened to "one of the most common reasons" (residual /r/ and /s/ errors are the classic referrals, but no source ranks r as THE single most common).
4. **"Auditory bombardment is one of the few home strategies with direct evidence behind it"**: overclaimed. Focused auditory input is a component of evidence-based approaches (e.g., Hodson's cycles), not an independently proven home program. Reworded to "a core ingredient of established therapy approaches such as cycles."
5. **Tongue tie FAQ**: strengthened with the actual evidence status. Chinnadurai et al. (2015), Pediatrics 135(6):e1467-e1474 (AHRQ-commissioned systematic review): no reliable evidence that ankyloglossia surgery improves speech. Added to sources and to the FAQ answer.

## Honest limits (kept visible on page)
- Process-elimination ages are textbook syntheses that vary by source; page says so now.
- "5 minutes of daily practice" is a practice recommendation grounded in distributed-practice motor-learning principles (Maas et al. 2008, added to sources), not a research-measured dose; presented as advice, not a statistic.
- Hustad milestones come from lab conditions (audio-only, zero context strangers), which is why parents understand far more; the FAQ already explains this.
