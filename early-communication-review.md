# Early Communication and Late Talkers page: accuracy review

## September 2026 pass: deep research, parent audits, readability

Second full pass. Every August verdict below was rechecked and stands. New sources were verified against the publisher record before being cited. Three parent-persona read-throughs were run against the page before editing; their findings and what was done about each are listed here so the reasoning is auditable.

### New evidence added (all verified this pass)
1. **Pointing as an early predictor now has a citation.** The August page called gestures "the strongest early predictor most families can actually observe" without a source. Lüke, Grimminger, Rohlfing, Liszkowski & Ritterfeld (2017), Child Development 88(2):484-492: index-finger pointing at 12 months predicted language at 24 months better than parent-report screening. Lüke, Ritterfeld & Liszkowski (2022), Frontiers in Pediatrics 10:878163, replicated it in 42 bilingual infants. Added to the 6-to-12-month callout and the reference list.
2. **Ear infections were overstated as a cause.** The page implied repeated infections and fluid explain late talking. Roberts, Rosenfeld & Zeisel (2004), Pediatrics 113(3):e238-e248, meta-analysis of prospective studies: no to very small associations between early otitis media with effusion and later speech and language. Rewritten in the hearing accordion and the causes list. A current hearing test is still recommended as the first thing to rule out, which is standard practice and unaffected by this finding.
3. **Parent-implemented intervention now has a second, larger meta-analysis.** Heidlage et al. (2020), Early Childhood Research Quarterly 50:6-23: 25 randomized trials, significant effects on expressive language, larger for children whose primary difficulty is language. Added beside Roberts & Kaiser (2011) in the coaching phase. Honest limit: receptive effects were not significant in that analysis, so the page attributes the receptive gain only to Roberts & Kaiser.
4. **Tongue-tie myth now cites the AAP 2024 clinical report.** Thomas et al., Pediatrics 154(2):e2024067605: frenotomy to prevent speech problems is not evidence-based; no evidence supports intervention on lip or cheek ties. Added to the myth answer.
5. **Bilingual vocabulary counting.** Core, Hoff, Rumiche & Señor (2013), JSLHR 56(5):1637-1649: total vocabulary across both languages tracked monolingual norms and identified the same proportion of children below the 25th percentile; single-language counts undercounted bilingual toddlers. Added to the bilingual FAQ so the page says how we count, not only that bilingualism is fine.
6. **"Wait and see" answer made more precise.** Rescorla & Dale (2013) and Rice et al. (2008) put persistence at around 20 percent of late talkers, consistent with the page's "a fifth to a quarter." The page now also states plainly that the evidence is stronger for early support building skills now than for it preventing a later language disorder. That is the honest version and it still favors acting.

# August 2026 review (retained)

Reviewed against ASHA guidance and primary research, same standard as `aac-review.md`. Claim-by-claim verdicts; page updated same day. Zero em dashes.

## Errors found and fixed

1. **Intelligibility at 18 to 24 months was wrong.** Page said a child is "understood by familiar adults about half the time." The 50 percent figure comes from the old Weiss/Lynch rule of thumb and describes *unfamiliar* listeners, not familiar ones. Hustad, Mahr, Natzke & Rathouz (2021), JSLHR 64(10):3707–3719 (538 typically developing children, 1,076 naive adult listeners) is the current normative reference. Replaced with a non-numeric statement.

2. **Intelligibility at age three was overstated.** Page said "understood by unfamiliar listeners most of the time by age three." Hustad 2021: 50 percent single-word intelligibility is reached at **31 months** at the 50th percentile; 75 percent is not reached until **49 months**. "Most of the time by three" is roughly a year and a half early. Replaced with the actual 31-month/50 percent benchmark.

3. **Internal contradiction on 18-month vocabulary.** Milestone list said "around 50 words by 18 months is typical" while the page's own Wordbank reference asserted a median near 90. Reworded to "a common benchmark, and it sits below the median rather than at it." Also removed the unverified 90-word figure from the Wordbank reference and kept only the well-established 24-month median (~300).

4. **Unattributed citation.** Reference 2 was "Published in the National Library of Medicine collection (PMC8219342)" with no author, year, or journal. Removed and replaced with Fisher (2017).

5. **Prevalence stat was invented.** Stat card read "15%." Zubrick et al. (2007), JSLHR 50(6):1562–1592, n=1,766 found **13.4%** on the ASQ composite criterion (19.1% using the single "combining words" item). ASHA's LLE portal cites a **10% to 20%** range across studies, 13.5% at 18 to 23 months, rising to 16% to 17.5% at 30 to 36 months. Card now reads 13% with both figures named. The redundant "2 word combinations" card was replaced with the 300-word median, which does more work.

6. **"Boys talk later" myth was answered with the wrong fact.** Page claimed only "a small average difference." Zubrick 2007 actually found **male sex to be a significant predictor** of late language emergence. The honest correction is stronger, not weaker: boys are more likely to start late, but Fisher (2017) found sex did **not** predict which late talkers caught up. Rewritten to say both.

7. **Catch-up claim had no numbers.** "A meaningful minority do not" replaced with: roughly a fifth to a quarter show persistent difficulty at school age; Fisher (2017) puts persistent difficulty at about **5 to 7 percent of the general population**; and Rescorla's longitudinal work showing that even resolved late talkers score modestly below peers as a group into adolescence. That last point was missing entirely and is the most commonly omitted honest caveat on pages like this.

8. **Wait-time claim softened.** "Adults typically wait one to two seconds" traces to classroom wait-time research, not toddler interaction. Reworded to "about a second" without a citation-shaped claim.

9. **4:1 comment-to-question ratio labeled honestly.** It is a Hanen-style clinical heuristic, not a research finding. Page now says so explicitly while defending the underlying principle.

10. **Sign language claim now carries the real figures.** Replaced "in study after study" with Millar, Light & Schlosser (2006): 89 percent gained speech, 11 percent no change, 0 percent declined, gains modest. Matches the AAC page's framing exactly.

## Added per your direction: the median vs average vs percentile problem

New section 02 block, **"Three different kinds of number, and why they disagree."** This is the single most common source of parent confusion on this topic and no competitor page explains it:

- **Median (50th percentile).** CDI and Wordbank report medians. Middle 24-month-old ≈ 300 words. Half of typical children are below it by definition, so being under the median is not a finding.
- **CDC benchmark (75th percentile).** Zubler et al. (2022), *Pediatrics* 149(3) revised the CDC checklists to report what **at least 75 percent** of children do by each age, replacing the older 50th-percentile standard. This is precisely why CDC lists 50 words at 30 months rather than 24. A missed CDC item puts a child in the bottom quarter.
- **Screening cut-point.** Fewer than 50 words or no combinations at 24 months, roughly bottom 10 to 15 percent, engineered to catch rather than describe.
- **Mean.** Rarely used, because the distribution is right-skewed and high talkers drag the mean above the median. An unsourced "average" figure online is usually a median wearing a disguise.

The FAQ answer and the schema.org FAQ entry were rewritten to match, so the structured data and the visible page agree.

## Verified and kept

- **50 words / two-word combinations at 24 months** as the screening line. Standard across Rescorla's LDS, Zubrick 2007, and ASHA. Kept.
- **Late talker definition** (18 to 30 months, expressive delay with typical comprehension, play, motor, and social skills). Matches ASHA's LLE definition. Kept.
- **Receptive language as a good prognostic sign.** Now strengthened rather than softened: Fisher (2017) found early receptive language among the strongest predictors of expressive outcomes.
- **No consonant babbling by ~10 months as a red flag.** Oller, Eilers, Neal & Cobo-Lewis (1998), AJMR 103(3):249–263. Kept.
- **Bilingualism does not cause delay.** Paradis, Genesee & Crago (2021). Kept.
- **Screen time.** Madigan et al. (2020), JAMA Pediatrics 174(7):665–675. Page correctly frames it as association plus displacement mechanism, not cause of most late talking. Kept.
- **Parent coaching.** Roberts & Kaiser (2011), AJSLP 20(3):180–199. Now cited inline in the coaching phase rather than only in the reference list.
- **Loss of skills at any age is time-sensitive.** Kept and left prominent.
- **No physician referral needed for private evaluation; no obligation to begin therapy.** Consistent with practice stance. Kept.

## Honest limits left visible on the page

- Prediction really does get more accurate after age three. The page now concedes this in the "should we wait" FAQ and answers it directly: accuracy improves by watching a child fall further behind.
- Late-talker outcome research is heterogeneous; definitions of "caught up" differ between studies. Page uses ranges, not point estimates.
- Hustad's intelligibility sample was 87 percent White and skewed to middle and upper-middle SES (the authors name this as a key limitation). The page uses the benchmark without overstating its generality.
