Understanding and using language

Understanding and Using Language: Directions, Questions, Sentences, and Stories

Language difficulty is the most common communication problem in children and the easiest to miss. It does not announce itself the way unclear speech does. It shows up as a child who seems not to be listening, who answers the wrong question, who takes forever to get a story out, or who is quietly falling behind in reading while decoding perfectly well.

This guide explains what language actually consists of, what difficulty looks like at each age, what developmental language disorder is, why it matters for reading and friendships, and the specific things families and teachers can do. It ends with how therapy works and how we approach it.

01Two sides of language

Understanding language and using language are separate skills.

Receptive language is what a child takes in: words, sentences, questions, instructions, stories, and the meaning behind them. Expressive language is what they put out: naming, describing, explaining, asking, narrating, and arguing. A child can be strong in one and struggling in the other, and the two need to be assessed separately because they need different help.

What language is made of

01

Vocabulary and word meaning

Not just how many words a child knows, but how well they know them. A deep word has category, function, features, opposites, related words, and multiple meanings attached to it. Shallow vocabulary knowledge is a common and often invisible cause of comprehension trouble later on.

02

Grammar and sentence structure

Word endings, verb tenses, pronouns, plurals, question forms, and the ability to combine ideas into longer sentences using words like because, although, and while. Grammatical difficulty is one of the most reliable markers of a language disorder in English-speaking children.

03

Comprehension of connected language

Understanding a paragraph is not the same as understanding each sentence in it. Following a story or a lesson requires holding information, linking it, inferring what was not said, and noticing when it stops making sense.

04

Narrative and organization

Telling a story someone else can follow requires knowing who, where, what happened, what went wrong, what was done about it, and how it ended, in an order that makes sense to a listener who was not there. Narrative skill is one of the stronger predictors of later reading and writing that can be measured in a young child.

05

Word finding

Knowing a word and retrieving it on demand are different. Word-finding difficulty looks like frequent um, thing, and you know, talking around a word, using vague language, or long pauses mid-sentence. It is frustrating and it is treatable.

06

Figurative and academic language

Idioms, sarcasm, jokes, inference, and the specialized language of instructions and textbooks. This layer becomes far more demanding around third and fourth grade, which is when many previously coping children start to struggle visibly.

02What it looks like

Signs by age

01

Toddlers and preschoolers

  • Slow vocabulary growth
  • Difficulty following simple directions
  • Short sentences compared with peers
  • Frequent grammatical errors past age four
  • Trouble answering who, what, and where questions
  • Difficulty learning songs, rhymes, and routines
  • Struggles to tell you about something that happened
  • Frustration or withdrawal during conversation
02

Early elementary

  • Only follows the first part of a two-step direction
  • Answers a different question than the one asked
  • Stories jump around and leave out key information
  • Vague language: thing, stuff, that guy
  • Difficulty learning new vocabulary from context
  • Slow to respond, or responds with a delay
  • Trouble with why and how questions
  • Written work far shorter than expected
  • Reading words accurately without understanding them
  • Described as not listening, or as needing constant reminders
03

Upper elementary and beyond

  • Comprehension drops as texts get harder
  • Struggles with idioms, sarcasm, and inference
  • Difficulty summarizing or paraphrasing
  • Trouble organizing a written paragraph
  • Misses instructions given verbally in class
  • Word problems in math are harder than the math
  • Avoids reading, writing, or speaking up
  • Social difficulty that appears around this age
  • Fatigue and frustration by the end of the school day
  • Grades slipping without an obvious cause

The common misreadChildren with language difficulty are frequently described as inattentive, unmotivated, oppositional, or shy. All four descriptions are what it looks like from outside when a child cannot reliably process what is being said or produce what they mean.

03Developmental language disorder

The most common childhood condition you have never heard of

Developmental language disorder, or DLD, is a persistent difficulty learning and using language that is not caused by another condition. It affects around seven percent of children, roughly two in an average classroom, which makes it far more common than many conditions with much higher public profiles. Most families have never had it named for them. If seven percent of children had a condition that affected their knees, everyone would know its name.

What DLD is not

  • It is not caused by low intelligence. Children with DLD have the full range of cognitive ability.
  • It is not caused by poor parenting, or by not being read to enough.
  • It is not caused by bilingualism, and it appears in all of a child's languages.
  • It is not laziness, and it is not something a child can try harder out of.
  • It is not the same as autism, though language difficulty can occur alongside it.
  • It is not usually outgrown, though the right support changes outcomes substantially.

“A language disorder is not a thinking problem. It is a difficulty with the system that gets thoughts in and out.”

That distinction matters enormously to a child who has spent years being told they are not applying themselves.

Language difference is not language disorder

A child learning English as an additional language, or speaking a dialect other than the one a test was built around, may score poorly on a standardized measure without having any disorder at all. This is one of the most common sources of both over-identification and under-identification, and a responsible evaluation is built to avoid it.

04Why it matters

Language runs underneath almost everything at school

01

Reading comprehension is language comprehension applied to print.

02

Writing requires organizing language before anything reaches the page.

03

Math becomes a language task as soon as it appears in a word problem.

04

Friendship runs on conversation, negotiation, humor, and storytelling.

The reading connection is worth dwelling on. A child can learn to decode beautifully and still not understand what they read, because decoding and comprehension are separate skills built on different foundations. This pattern often stays hidden until around third grade, when texts stop being simple and the gap opens quickly.

There is also a well-documented association between unidentified language difficulty and later emotional and behavioral difficulty. A child who cannot follow what is happening, cannot explain themselves, and gets in trouble for both, will eventually conclude something about themselves. Naming the actual problem is protective in itself.

05What helps at home

Strategies that change how language lands

01

Cut the instruction in half

Long instructions overload working memory, and the child typically holds onto the last piece. Give one step, wait for it to be done, then give the next. Add a visual or a gesture wherever you can.

Go upstairs, get your shoes, and put your book in your bagtryFirst, shoes. (pause) Now, book in bag.
02

Give ten seconds before you rephrase

Rephrasing feels supportive and is often counterproductive, because the child has to abandon the sentence they were processing and start over on a new one. Say it once, clearly, and then be quiet. If you do need to repeat, repeat the same words rather than new ones.

03

Check understanding without asking whether they understood

“Do you understand?” reliably produces a yes, whether or not it is true. It may be the only question in English with a fixed answer. Ask the child to tell you what they are going to do, or what the first step is. That reveals what actually got through, and it costs ten seconds.

04

Teach words in networks, not lists

A word that is stored with connections is easier to retrieve and easier to understand. When a new word comes up, attach it to what it is, what it does, what group it belongs to, what it looks like, what it is similar to, and what it is not.

  • What group does it belong to?
  • What does it do, or what is it used for?
  • What does it look, sound, or feel like?
  • Where would you find it?
  • What is it similar to?
  • What is the opposite?
05

Recast grammar rather than correcting it

When a child says he goed to the park, reply he went to the park? What did he do there? The correct form is modeled immediately, at the point the child is most invested, without a correction to absorb. Do this consistently and it works: in the pooled research, interventions built on recasting improved grammar targets by close to a full standard deviation.

06

Build stories with a frame

Narrative is teachable, and a consistent frame gives a child something to hang the pieces on. Use the same questions every time, at dinner, in the car, after school.

  • Who was there?
  • Where were you?
  • What happened first?
  • What went wrong, or what was the problem?
  • What did you do about it?
  • How did it end, and how did you feel?

Try thisTell your own day using the same frame first. Modeling a complete story is more useful than interrogating a child for one.

07

Climb the question ladder

Question types have a developmental order and vary enormously in difficulty. What and who are concrete and visible. Why and how require reasoning about things that are not in front of the child. Meet them where they are and move up gradually.

1What and whoConcrete, visible, one right answer.
2Where and whenRequires holding a mental picture.
3Why and howRequires reasoning, sequencing, and inference.
08

Read together, and talk about it more than you read it

Shared reading is the single richest language activity available to families, and it works best as a conversation rather than a performance. Stop and wonder aloud. Predict. Ask what a character is thinking. Connect it to your child's life. Explain one or two new words properly rather than twenty in passing.

This continues to be valuable long after a child can read independently. Reading aloud to an older child exposes them to sentence structures and vocabulary well beyond their own reading level.

09

Give word-finding tools

When a word will not come, supplying it ends the struggle but teaches nothing. Instead, prompt with a strategy the child can eventually use alone.

  • What group is it in?
  • What does it do?
  • What sound does it start with?
  • Describe it and I will guess
  • Where do you see it?
10

Sportscast, do not interrogate

The car ride home is a poor time for twenty questions, especially for a child whose language is already tired. Narrate instead: talk about your own day, describe what you see, think aloud. Children join a commentary far more readily than they answer a quiz.

11

Build knowledge, not just words

Comprehension depends heavily on what a child already knows about a topic. Museums, cooking, gardening, documentaries, taking things apart, and talking about how things work all feed language, because vocabulary attaches to experience much better than to definitions.

12

Teach the self-advocacy sentences

A child who can say can you say that a different way has a tool for the rest of their life. Practice the phrases at home until they are automatic, then agree with the teacher that using them is welcome.

  • Can you say that again?
  • Can you say it a different way?
  • What does that word mean?
  • Can I have a minute to think?
  • Can you write it down?
  • What was the first step?
06Support at school

Accommodations worth asking for

Most of these cost a teacher nothing and help the whole class. They can be discussed informally with a teacher, or written into an IEP or 504 Plan where a child qualifies.

Instructions broken into single steps Extra time to process and respond Written instructions alongside spoken ones Visual schedules and checklists Key vocabulary taught before the lesson Graphic organizers for writing Comprehension checked, not assumed Warning before being called on Seating near the teacher Reduced language load on tests Access to notes or a note-taking partner Permission to ask for repetition without penalty

On sharing an outside report. A private evaluation gives you clinical documentation and specific recommendations to bring to a school meeting. Schools run their own eligibility process and make their own decisions, but arriving with a detailed description of your child's language profile tends to make that conversation far more concrete.

07What therapy looks like

How language therapy works

Language therapy targets specific structures and skills, embedded in activities that require them for a real purpose. Worksheets are rarely the point. The evidence favors working within meaningful contexts, with high numbers of well-designed opportunities to hear and use the target.

01

Grammar and sentence structure

Specific targets such as past tense, pronouns, question forms, or complex sentence connectors, taught through concentrated exposure, recasting, and structured practice in conversation and play rather than in isolated drills.

02

Vocabulary depth

Fewer words, known better. We teach word networks, multiple meanings, and word parts such as prefixes and roots, which give the child a way to work out new words independently rather than needing each one taught.

03

Narrative intervention

Explicit teaching of story structure using icons, retelling, and personal storytelling. This is one of the highest-yield areas in school-age language therapy because it feeds comprehension, writing, and social conversation at the same time.

04

Comprehension and inference

Teaching a child to notice when meaning has broken down, to ask for clarification, to make predictions, and to use clues in the text and their own background knowledge to fill gaps the writer left deliberately.

05

Following directions and working memory strategies

Chunking, visualizing, rehearsing, and asking for repetition, practiced until they become habits the child uses without being prompted. The aim is independence, not compliance.

06

Curriculum-linked language

Where possible we use the child's own books, assignments, and classroom vocabulary as therapy material, so that gains show up where they are needed rather than only in the session.

How Social House Therapy approaches language

01
We look at both sides carefully

Comprehension is easy to overestimate, so we assess it in ways that strip out routine and context. Understanding is often where the real work is.

02
Language difference is not treated as disorder

Evaluations account for the languages and dialects a child uses, and we weigh standardized scores accordingly rather than taking them at face value.

03
Goals connect to the classroom

Targets are chosen for what they unlock: following the lesson, writing the paragraph, telling the story, joining the conversation.

04
Children get an explanation too

Older children are told plainly what is hard for them and why. Understanding that it is a language difference, not a lack of ability, changes how a child sees themselves.

Language difficulties are the ones that most often arrive at my door with a story attached: the child is not trying, the child is not listening, the child is being difficult.
