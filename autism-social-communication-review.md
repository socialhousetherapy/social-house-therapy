# Autism & Social Communication page: accuracy review (August 2026)

Reviewed against the ASHA Practice Portal (Autism; Social Communication Disorder), the NJC, and primary research. Claim-by-claim verdicts; page updated same day. Same process as aac-review.md.

## Verified and kept (now cited more precisely)
1. **"Autistic children are usually interested in connection."** ASHA's Autism Practice Portal states individuals with ASD "report a desire to have friendships and relationships." Page now says so explicitly. Kept.
2. **Double empathy / misunderstanding runs both ways.** Milton (2012), Disability & Society 27(6):883–887 (concept); Crompton et al. (2020), Autism 24(7):1704–1712: diffusion-chain ("telephone") study: autistic chains transferred information as accurately as non-autistic chains; accuracy dropped only in mixed chains. Concrete finding added to the page.
3. **Echolalia is communicative.** Prizant & Duchan (1981), JSHD 46(3):241–249 (functions of immediate echolalia) and Prizant & Rydell (1984), JSHR 27(2):183–192 (functions of delayed echolalia) are the empirical basis for the page's "what a gestalt might be doing" list; Stiegler (2015), AJSLP 24(4):750–762 reviews the literature. Both function studies added to sources.
4. **Masking.** Pearson & Rose (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(1):52–60. Added Hull et al. (2017), JADD 47(8):2519–2534 ("putting on my best normal": phrase now quoted on page). ASHA's portal separately notes camouflaging in autistic girls (Dean et al. 2017; Tierney et al. 2016). Kept.
5. **Stimming is regulation.** Kapp et al. (2019), Autism 23(7):1782–1792: 32 autistic adults; thematic analysis found stimming is a self-regulatory mechanism; authors argue against suppressing non-injurious stims. Added to sources.
6. **Eye contact stance.** Trevisan et al. (2017), PLOS ONE 12(11):e0188446: first-hand accounts describe adverse emotional/physiological reactions, invasion, sensory overload. Hadjikhani et al. (2017), Scientific Reports 7:3163: constraining gaze to the eye region produced abnormally high subcortical (arousal-circuit) activation in autistic participants; authors warn forced eye contact in therapy can create anxiety. Both added to sources; FAQ answer strengthened. ASHA's portal also cautions that eye-contact norms are culturally variable.
7. **AAC claims.** Same evidence base verified for aac-support.html: Millar, Light & Schlosser (2006) best-evidence subset: 89% gained speech, 11% no change, 0% decreased, gains modest; Schlosser & Wendt (2008) autism-specific. Page now states the 89%/0% figures (it previously said only "research consistently shows") and links to the AAC guide.
8. **"No diagnosis required" / "never too late."** ASHA's portal explicitly rejects a priori eligibility criteria: chronological age ("too young"/"too old"), cognitive referencing, diagnostic label, absence of prerequisite skills, failure to benefit from previous services. FAQ now cites the age point plainly.
9. **Presume competence.** Framed via Donnellan (1984), Behavioral Disorders 9(2):141–150: "the criterion of the least dangerous assumption." Named on page; added to sources.
10. **Play-based, child-led therapy is mainstream, not fringe.** Schreibman et al. (2015), JADD 45(8):2411–2428 (NDBI as empirically validated); Sandbank et al. (2020) Project AIM already cited. Sentence added to therapy section; Schreibman added to sources.
11. **Social skills groups caution.** Gates, Kang & Lerner (2017), Clinical Psychology Review 52:164–181: 19 RCTs, overall g=0.51; self-report large (0.92), task-based medium (0.58), parent/observer small (0.47/0.40), **teacher-report: no significant effect**. Supports the page's generalization concern; figures now paraphrased in the FAQ. Kept "cautious," not "never."
12. **No discredited methods.** Page recommends nothing ASHA has position statements against (FC, RPM, AIT all absent). Confirmed.

## Fixed
1. **"5x: modeling ratio on AAC" counter**: the same invented-sounding figure removed from the AAC page in that review; it had survived here. Replaced with the verified Millar 89% figure.
2. **Gestalt six-stage framework presented as settled science.** It is not. Blanc's NLA (2012) is a clinical framework; Prizant himself wrote his stages carried "no claims... as to their psychological reality." Hutchins, Knox & Fletcher (2024), Autism & Dev. Language Impairments 9 (doi 10.1177/23969415241249944) challenge the stages' validity; Bryant et al. (2024) systematic review found no intervention evidence; ASHA SIG commentaries (2025) note some NLA strategies align with evidence-based practice while others lack support. ASHA's own portal still lists Blanc (2012) as a view on echolalia's role, so presenting the framework is defensible: presenting it as proven is not. Page now: softened "both routes lead" to "can end in," added a "Where the science stands, honestly" aside, labeled the map as Blanc's clinical framework, cited Hutchins in sources, and committed to never using stages to delay AAC or gate services. Schema FAQ answers softened to match ("early step toward flexible language" rather than "first stage of").
3. **Vague research claims made precise.** "Research consistently shows" (AAC) → exact 89%/0% + modest-gains caveat, in body, FAQ, and schema. Double-empathy claim → concrete Crompton chain finding.

## Honest limits (kept visible on page)
- The six-stage GLP map is a clinical lens, not validated stages; the page says so and cites the critique.
- AAC speech gains were modest (Millar 2006); page says "voice, not a speech cure."
- Social-groups evidence is mixed rather than damning (medium overall effect); page stays at "cautious."
- Regulation-first is clinical consensus (SCERTS tradition) rather than an RCT-proven claim; page frames it as practice, not a statistic.
- Declarative language, wait time, and visual supports are framed as strategies, not cited statistics.
